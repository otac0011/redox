# Skin & brain: oxidative stress where you can see and feel it

*Two organs where oxidative stress has visible or felt consequences: skin, where sunlight drives oxidative damage you can watch accumulate in the mirror, and the brain, the textbook "oxidatively vulnerable" organ. Literature checked 2026-10-01. Every PMID was pulled from PubMed during this session and checked for retraction status (none of the cited papers is retracted). Factor ids appear in square brackets.*

## Summary

- **Skin: the best antioxidant is a filter, not an antioxidant.** Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen is the only intervention with long-term randomized evidence against oxidative skin aging: 24% less photoaging over 4.5 years [skb-hughes2013], 39% fewer squamous-cell tumours [skb-green1999], and half as many melanomas over the following decade [skb-green2011]. See [sunscreen].
- **Topical retinoids are the best-proven repair treatment** for photoaged skin [skb-weiss1988, skb-kafi2007]. They work by blocking the UV-triggered, ROS-driven enzymes that chew up collagen [skb-fisher1997], not by scavenging radicals. See [topical-retinoids].
- **Topical vitamin C works only when formulated properly** (L-ascorbic acid at pH below 3.5, about 10-20%) [skb-pinnell2001], and its clinical evidence is small [skb-correia2023]. See [topical-vitamin-c].
- **Nicotinamide 500 mg twice daily** cut new non-melanoma skin cancers by 23% in high-risk people [skb-chen2015] but did nothing in transplant recipients [skb-allen2023]. See [nicotinamide].
- **Antioxidant pills do not protect skin.** Beta-carotene did nothing for photoaging or skin cancer in the same Nambour trial [skb-hughes2013, skb-green1999], and a modest antioxidant cocktail raised skin cancer in women in SU.VI.MAX [skb-hercberg2007]. Dietary tomato paste gives a small, slow rise in sunburn resistance [skb-stahl2001, skb-rizwan2011].
- **Brain: a strong mechanistic case, a graveyard of antioxidant trials.** The brain is oxidatively vulnerable [skb-cobley2018, skb-halliwell2006], and oxidative damage appears early in Alzheimer disease [skb-nunomura2001]. Yet vitamin E, CoQ10, urate-raising inosine and iron chelation have all failed or harmed in neurodegeneration trials [skb-petersen2005, skb-kryscio2017, skb-beal2014, skb-schwarzschild2021, skb-devos2022, skb-ayton2025]. The one partial exception is vitamin E 2000 IU/day modestly slowing functional decline in Alzheimer disease [skb-dysken2014].
- **Diet and cognition:** cocoa flavanols failed their primary cognitive end points in COSMOS [skb-baker2023, skb-vyas2024b, skb-brickman2023], the MIND diet failed its RCT [skb-barnes2023], and omega-3 supplements have consistently null trials [skb-burckhardt2016, skb-chew2015, skb-andrieu2017, skb-kang2022]. A plain multivitamin produced small but replicated memory benefits within COSMOS [skb-baker2023, skb-yeung2023, skb-vyas2024a], although an earlier long trial was null [skb-grodstein2013]. B vitamins slowed brain atrophy only in people with mild cognitive impairment and raised homocysteine [skb-smith2010].
- **What protects the brain best is unglamorous:** treating cardiovascular risk, hearing loss, air pollution and heavy drinking (the Lancet Commission's 14 modifiable risk factors account for about 45% of dementia [skb-livingston2024]), plus exercise and adequate sleep, each with its own caveats.

## Part 1: Skin

### 1.1 How sunlight oxidizes skin

Less than 10% of the solar radiation reaching skin is ultraviolet [skb-liebel2012], but it does most of the damage. The two UV bands damage skin in different ways (see [uv-sun-exposure]):

- **UVB (290-320 nm)** is absorbed directly by DNA in the epidermis and fuses neighbouring bases into cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs). This is the classic sunburn and skin-cancer waveband.
- **UVA (320-400 nm)** is much more abundant than UVB [skb-mouret2006] and penetrates into the dermis. DNA absorbs it poorly; instead, other molecules in the cell absorb it and pass the energy to oxygen, making singlet oxygen and superoxide. These oxidize guanine to 8-oxo-dG, peroxidize membrane lipids and damage mitochondrial DNA. A careful mass-spectrometry study in whole human skin added a twist: UVA also produced CPDs, in larger amounts than 8-oxo-dG, and these were repaired more slowly than UVB-induced dimers. Skin protected its cells well against UVB but only weakly against UVA [skb-mouret2006].

**From ROS to wrinkles.** Fisher and colleagues irradiated volunteers' buttock skin and took biopsies. A single UV exposure switched on three collagen-degrading matrix metalloproteinases (collagenase/MMP-1, a 92 kDa gelatinase and stromelysin) and increased breakdown of type I collagen by 58%. With four exposures two days apart, enzyme activity stayed elevated for a week. Pretreatment with tretinoin blocked MMP induction by 70-80% [skb-fisher1997]. ROS act here mainly as **signals**: they activate MAP kinases and the AP-1 transcription factor, which turns on MMP genes. Each sub-burning exposure leaves a little more fragmented collagen behind, and decades of this produce photoaging: coarse wrinkles, laxity and blotchy pigment.

This also explains why skin aging tracks sun habits so closely in **identical twins**: in 186 twin pairs, the twin with more sun exposure, more outdoor activity and less sunscreen looked older [skb-guyuron2009].

### 1.2 Beyond UV: visible light and infrared

Cosmetic marketing increasingly sells "visible light" and "infrared" protection. The evidence is real but mostly laboratory-level:

| Waveband | What has been shown | Evidence level |
|---|---|---|
| Visible light (400-700 nm) | Induced ROS, cytokines and MMP-1 in skin equivalents and free-radical activity in human skin; ordinary sunscreens barely blocked it [skb-liebel2012] | Mechanistic (industry lab) |
| Visible light in melasma | Iron-oxide (tinted) sunscreen improved melasma 15-28% more than UV-only sunscreen over 8 weeks [skb-castanedo2014] | One RCT; relevant for darker skin and pigment disorders |
| Near-infrared (IR-A) | Raised dermal MMP-1 in 80% of volunteers and depleted skin antioxidants; topical antioxidants prevented it [skb-schroeder2008, skb-gretherbeck2015] | Mechanistic and molecular-marker RCT |
| IR-A in real life | Annual solar IR doses are similar to those of furnace workers, who show little chronic skin damage [skb-diffey2016] | Critical appraisal |

Bottom line: visible-light protection (tinted mineral sunscreens) is justified for melasma and hyperpigmentation; infrared protection for photoaging is an unproven claim.

### 1.3 Sunscreen: the RCT evidence

The Nambour trial in Queensland, Australia, randomized 1621 adults to daily broad-spectrum SPF 15+ sunscreen on the head, neck, arms and hands, or to their usual discretionary use, and separately to 30 mg/day beta-carotene or placebo, for 4.5 years (1992-1996).

| Outcome | Result | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Photoaging (skin microtopography, adults under 55) | No detectable aging in the daily group; 24% less aging than discretionary users (relative odds 0.76, 0.59-0.98) | [skb-hughes2013] |
| Squamous-cell carcinoma tumours | 39% fewer (rate ratio 0.61) | [skb-green1999] |
| Basal-cell carcinoma | No effect | [skb-green1999] |
| Melanoma, 10 years after the trial | 11 vs 22 cases (HR 0.50, P=0.051); invasive 3 vs 11 (HR 0.27) | [skb-green2011] |
| Beta-carotene 30 mg/day | No effect on photoaging or either skin cancer | [skb-hughes2013, skb-green1999] |

**Two safety questions.** First, organic filters do get into the blood: all six common filters exceeded the FDA's 0.5 ng/mL threshold after one application, with oxybenzone reaching about 258 ng/mL under maximal use [skb-matta2020]. That triggers toxicology testing; it is not evidence of harm. Second, vitamin D: an expert consensus concluded that sunscreen used to prevent sunburn is unlikely to compromise vitamin D status [skb-passeron2019].

### 1.4 Topical antioxidants: vitamin C, vitamin E and ferulic acid

Skin is the one organ where you can put an antioxidant exactly where the ROS are generated, so topical antioxidants make more sense than pills. But delivery is everything:

- **Penetration:** in pig skin, L-ascorbic acid entered only at pH below 3.5. Absorption peaked at 20%, tissue levels saturated after three daily applications and fell with a half-life of about 4 days. The popular "stable" derivatives (magnesium ascorbyl phosphate, ascorbyl palmitate) did not raise skin vitamin C at all [skb-pinnell2001].
- **Synergy:** 15% L-ascorbic acid plus 1% alpha-tocopherol protected pig skin against UV about 4-fold, better than either alone [skb-lin2003]. Adding ferulic acid stabilized the solution and doubled protection to about 8-fold, also reducing thymine dimers [skb-lin2005]. All three formulation papers come from the same Duke group (Pinnell and colleagues), not from independent labs [skb-pinnell2001, skb-lin2003, skb-lin2005].
- **Clinical trials:** a 6-month double-blind trial of 5% vitamin C cream improved wrinkles, skin microrelief and elastic tissue versus vehicle [skb-humbert2003]. The only independent systematic review found 7 RCTs with 139 participants in total, showing smoother skin and lighter pigment [skb-correia2023]. A Bayesian pooling of 31 in-house L'Oreal studies found dose-dependent prevention of UV-induced pigmentation [skb-dedormael2019].

A protection factor of 8 in pig skin is well below a typical sunscreen. Topical vitamin C is a reasonable add-on under sunscreen, mainly for pigment and fine texture. It is not a sunscreen substitute.

### 1.5 Topical retinoids: the strongest repair evidence

In a double-blind trial, tretinoin reversed signs of photoaging: all 30 treated forearms improved, as did 14 of 15 treated faces, versus none of the vehicle-treated faces [skb-weiss1988]. Over-the-counter retinol 0.4% reduced fine wrinkling and increased procollagen and glycosaminoglycans even in 87-year-olds [skb-kafi2007]. The mechanism ties back to oxidative signalling: tretinoin blocks the UV/ROS-driven MMP surge [skb-fisher1997].

Two cautions. In the 1131-patient VA trial, high-dose tretinoin did **not** prevent basal- or squamous-cell carcinoma [skb-weinstock2012], and the trial stopped early because of excess deaths in the tretinoin group, judged by the investigators as unlikely to be causal [skb-weinstock2009]. In pregnancy, a prospective cohort found no excess malformations after first-trimester topical tretinoin [skb-loureiro2005], but avoidance remains the precautionary norm. See [topical-retinoids].

### 1.6 Nicotinamide: a vitamin for DNA repair, not an antioxidant

Nicotinamide (the amide form of vitamin B3, not flushing niacin) feeds NAD+, the fuel for DNA repair after UV. In ONTRAC, 386 people with at least two prior non-melanoma skin cancers took 500 mg twice daily or placebo for a year. New skin cancers fell 23%, squamous-cell carcinomas about 30%, and actinic keratoses 11-20%. The benefit vanished within 6 months of stopping [skb-chen2015]. A meta-analysis found skin cancers halved overall (rate ratio 0.50), with heterogeneity explained by risk of bias [skb-mainville2022].

Then came the trial in organ-transplant recipients, the highest-risk group of all: 207 versus 210 new keratinocyte cancers, rate ratio 1.0 [skb-allen2023]. It stopped early with only 158 participants. A Veterans Affairs cohort of more than 33,000 people found 14% lower risk overall and 54% when started after a first skin cancer, but no overall benefit in transplant recipients [skb-breglio2025].

Topical 5% niacinamide is a separate, cosmetic use: in a 50-woman split-face trial run by Procter & Gamble it reduced fine lines, spots and blotchiness over 12 weeks [skb-bissett2005]. See [nicotinamide].

### 1.7 Eating for your skin: carotenoids, Polypodium, and the pill warning

**Carotenoids from food.** Tomato paste (40 g/day, about 16 mg lycopene, with olive oil) reduced UV-induced redness by 40% after 10 weeks, with no effect at 4 weeks [skb-stahl2001]. In a 20-woman RCT, 55 g/day tomato paste for 12 weeks raised the burn threshold and reduced UV-induced MMP-1 and mitochondrial DNA damage [skb-rizwan2011]. A meta-analysis of 21 trials supports a consistent but small effect [skb-zhang2024]. Beta-carotene supplements protect against sunburn only after at least 10 weeks [skb-kopcke2008]. The effect is small, takes weeks to appear, and is no substitute for sunscreen; see [tomatoes-lycopene].

**Polypodium leucotomos.** A single 7.5 mg/kg dose of this fern extract reduced UV redness, sunburn cells and DNA dimers in 9 volunteers [skb-middelkamphup2004]. A 2025 systematic review found 21 small studies across six skin conditions [skb-zundell2025]. It is an adjunct at best; see [polypodium-leucotomos].

**Antioxidant pills: the warning.** In Nambour, 30 mg/day beta-carotene did nothing for photoaging or skin cancer [skb-hughes2013, skb-green1999]. In the French SU.VI.MAX trial of 13,017 adults, a capsule of nutritional doses of vitamin C, vitamin E, beta-carotene, selenium and zinc raised skin cancer in women (HR 1.68), including melanoma (HR 4.31, few cases), with no effect in men [skb-hercberg2007]. This fits the wider antioxidant-supplement record ([high-dose-antioxidant-cocktail], [beta-carotene-supplement]).

### 1.8 Smoking, alcohol, pollution and sleep

| Exposure | Skin finding | Source | Quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smoking | In identical twins, the smoker had worse eyelid bags, nasolabial folds, lip wrinkles and jowls | [skb-okada2013, skb-guyuron2009] | Twin comparison (strong design for an observational study) |
| Alcohol | Heavy drinking was associated with worse under-eye puffiness, midface volume loss and visible blood vessels in 3267 women | [skb-goodman2019] | Self-rated survey, funded by Allergan |
| Traffic pollution | More soot exposure was associated with about 20% more facial pigment spots in older women | [skb-vierkotter2010] | Cohort |
| Poor sleep | Poor sleepers had higher intrinsic aging scores, 30% slower barrier recovery and slower recovery from UV redness | [skb-oyetakinwhite2015] | Small cross-sectional (60 women) |
| One night without sleep | Faces rated as having darker circles, paler skin and more fine lines | [skb-sundelin2013] | Experimental, short-term appearance only |

Smoking is the clearest non-UV driver of skin aging ([tobacco-smoking]). The alcohol and sleep data are weak, and should be weighed by their whole-body effects ([alcohol], [short-sleep]).

## Part 2: Brain

### 2.1 Why the brain is oxidatively vulnerable

The brain uses about 20% of the body's resting oxygen [skb-cobley2018]. Cobley and colleagues list "13 reasons" it is susceptible to oxidative stress, including membranes rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids (DHA and arachidonic acid, the most peroxidizable lipids), dense mitochondria, calcium- and glutamate-driven ROS bursts, redox-active iron and copper, auto-oxidizing neurotransmitters such as dopamine, and only modest antioxidant enzyme defences [skb-cobley2018, skb-halliwell2006]. They also stress that ROS are needed for normal brain signalling, such as synaptic plasticity, which is one reason blunt antioxidant loading may not help.

### 2.2 Oxidative stress in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's

In Alzheimer disease, neuronal RNA oxidation and protein nitration are greatest **early** and decline as amyloid accumulates, suggesting oxidative damage precedes plaques and tangles [skb-nunomura2001]. Oxidative damage to energy-metabolism enzymes may explain the sharp fall in brain glucose use seen on PET [skb-butterfield2019]. In Parkinson's disease, dopamine oxidation and iron in the substantia nigra make the same argument. The problem is that a pathway being *involved* does not mean an antioxidant can *fix* it.

### 2.3 The antioxidant trials: a long run of failures

| Trial | Intervention | Population | Result | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ADCS 1997 | Vitamin E 2000 IU/day | Moderate AD (341) | Benefit only after adjusting for a baseline imbalance | [skb-sano1997] |
| ADCS MCI 2005 | Vitamin E 2000 IU/day | MCI (769) | No delay in progression to AD (HR 1.02) | [skb-petersen2005] |
| TEAM-AD 2014 | Vitamin E 2000 IU/day | Mild-moderate AD (561) | Functional decline 19%/year slower (about 6 months' delay); no cognitive benefit | [skb-dysken2014] |
| Cochrane 2017 | Vitamin E | AD and MCI | No clinically important cognitive benefit | [skb-farina2017] |
| PREADViSE 2017 | Vitamin E 400 IU and/or selenium 200 ug | 7540 healthy men | No dementia prevention | [skb-kryscio2017] |
| WACS 2009 | Vitamin E, C, beta-carotene | 2824 women 65+ | No effect on cognitive change | [skb-kang2009] |
| PHS II 2007 | Beta-carotene 50 mg alternate days | 5956 men | Tiny benefit only after about 18 years | [skb-grodstein2007] |
| ADCS 2012 | Vitamin E + C + alpha-lipoic acid | AD (78) | CSF isoprostanes fell 19%, but cognitive decline was faster | [skb-galasko2012] |
| DATATOP 1993 | Vitamin E 2000 IU/day | Early PD (800) | No benefit | [skb-psg1993] |
| QE3 2014 | CoQ10 1200-2400 mg/day | Early PD (600) | Stopped for futility, slight adverse trend | [skb-beal2014], after the positive pilot [skb-shults2002] |
| SURE-PD3 2021 | Inosine to raise urate 44% | Early PD (298) | No benefit, more kidney stones | [skb-schwarzschild2021] |
| FAIRPARK-II 2022 | Iron chelator deferiprone | Early PD (372) | Lowered nigral iron, **worsened** parkinsonism | [skb-devos2022] |

The Galasko trial is the clearest illustration of the antioxidant paradox: the treatment demonstrably lowered a brain oxidative-stress marker and still made cognition worse [skb-galasko2012]. The iron-chelation trials show the same: removing a Fenton-chemistry catalyst from the brain harmed patients [skb-devos2022, skb-ayton2025]. CoQ10 ([coq10]) and vitamin E ([vitamin-e-supplement]) should not be taken for brain protection.

### 2.4 Diet and cognition

**Cocoa flavanols and multivitamins: the COSMOS trials.** COSMOS randomized older Americans to cocoa extract (500 mg flavanols/day) and/or a standard multivitamin. Its three cognitive substudies gave consistent answers:

| Substudy | Cocoa extract | Multivitamin | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| COSMOS-Mind (2262, phone testing, 3 years) | No effect on global cognition | +0.07 SD global cognition | [skb-baker2023] |
| COSMOS-Web (3562, online testing) | Primary memory end point null; benefit only in people with poor diet quality or low flavanol intake | Better immediate recall, "3.1 years" of memory aging | [skb-brickman2023, skb-yeung2023] |
| COSMOS-Clinic (573, in person, 2 years) | No effect | Episodic memory benefit; pooled 0.07 SD across substudies | [skb-vyas2024b, skb-vyas2024a] |
| Incident MCI/dementia (COSMOS-Mind) | No reduction | No reduction (low power) | [skb-sachs2023] |

The cocoa result matters for [cocoa-flavanols]: their vascular benefits are well documented, but they did not improve cognition in the general older population. An earlier 37-person trial suggesting flavanols boost dentate-gyrus memory function [skb-brickman2014] was not borne out at scale, except in the subgroup with low habitual intake [skb-brickman2023]. Two authors of the COSMOS-Web flavanol paper were Mars employees [skb-brickman2023], and Mars supported COSMOS, although the investigators state it had no role in design or analysis [skb-baker2023]. The multivitamin finding is small (about 0.07 SD) and contrasts with the null 12-year Physicians' Health Study II [skb-grodstein2013]; see [multivitamin-mineral].

**MIND and Mediterranean diets.** The MIND diet was built from the Rush Memory and Aging Project, where the highest adherence tertile had 53% lower Alzheimer incidence [skb-morris2015], and an Australian cohort found similar odds [skb-hosking2019]. A 20-year Swedish cohort of 28,025 people found no link between midlife diet quality and dementia [skb-glans2023]. The decisive test, a 3-year RCT in 604 older adults, found no difference in cognition or MRI between MIND and a control diet with the same mild calorie restriction; both groups improved [skb-barnes2023]. A PREDIMED substudy did find better cognitive composites with a Mediterranean diet plus olive oil or nuts versus a low-fat diet [skb-vallspedret2015]. See [mind-diet] and [mediterranean-diet].

**Blueberries and anthocyanins.** In the Nurses' Health Study, women eating the most berries had cognitive decline delayed by up to 2.5 years [skb-devore2012]. Trials are small: 9 people in an uncontrolled pilot [skb-krikorian2010], 37 adults on 24 g/day freeze-dried blueberry (about 1 cup fresh) with fewer memory errors [skb-miller2018], and a 6-month trial in mild decline showing faster processing speed [skb-cheatham2023]. Meta-analyses split: one found improved episodic memory in older adults with MCI or subjective decline (SMD 0.34) [skb-dasilva2025]; another of 14 RCTs in healthy people found better blood-vessel function but no cognitive benefit [skb-he2026]. See [blueberries].

**Omega-3 and cognition (a note, not a new factor).** The exposure is the same as [omega3-fish] (oily fish and fish-oil EPA/DHA), so the cognition evidence is summarized here rather than in a separate factor. Observationally, each extra weekly fish serving is associated with about 5% lower dementia risk [skb-zhang2016]. Randomized trials are consistently null: Cochrane found no benefit in Alzheimer disease (high-quality evidence) [skb-burckhardt2016], and DHA 2 g/day did not slow decline in Alzheimer disease [skb-quinn2010]. Prevention trials were null too: AREDS2 (5 years) [skb-chew2015], MAPT (3 years) [skb-andrieu2017] and VITAL (1 g/day, 2-3 years) [skb-kang2022]. The live hypotheses are that benefit is confined to the predementia stage or APOE4 carriers [skb-yassine2017], or depends on adequate B-vitamin status (see below) [skb-jerneren2015]. Eating fish is reasonable for many reasons; fish-oil capsules for memory are not supported.

**B vitamins and homocysteine.** In VITACOG, 168 people over 70 with mild cognitive impairment took folic acid 0.8 mg, B6 20 mg and B12 0.5 mg or placebo for 2 years. Brain atrophy slowed from 1.08% to 0.76% per year, and by 53% in those with homocysteine above 13 umol/L [skb-smith2010]. Atrophy in Alzheimer-vulnerable grey matter was reduced up to seven-fold in that subgroup [skb-douaud2013], and cognitive benefit appeared only above median homocysteine [skb-dejager2012]. Strikingly, benefit was confined to people with good baseline omega-3 levels [skb-jerneren2015, skb-oulhaj2016]. Against this: an 11-trial meta-analysis of about 22,000 people found no cognitive effect [skb-clarke2014], and high-dose B vitamins did not help established Alzheimer disease [skb-aisen2008]. A consensus group nevertheless classifies raised homocysteine as a modifiable dementia risk factor [skb-smith2018]. See [b-vitamins-cognition].

### 2.5 Lifestyle and environment

**Exercise.** In a 120-person RCT, a year of walking increased anterior hippocampal volume by 2%, reversing 1-2 years of age-related shrinkage, while the stretching controls lost volume [skb-erickson2011]. A meta-analysis of 14 trials found no significant effect on total hippocampal volume, only preservation of the left hippocampus [skb-firth2018]. Hard cognitive outcomes are disappointing: the 2-year LIFE trial in 1635 sedentary older adults found no cognitive benefit [skb-sink2015], and in people who already had dementia, intensive exercise slightly *worsened* cognition [skb-lamb2018]. Exercise remains strongly justified for cardiovascular and metabolic reasons ([aerobic-exercise-moderate]); its cognitive benefit is real at the level of brain structure in some trials, but small or absent on cognitive tests.

**Sleep and "brain washing".** The glymphatic hypothesis came from mice, where sleep expanded the brain's interstitial space by 60% and sped clearance of amyloid [skb-xie2013]. Human data are suggestive but indirect: one night of sleep deprivation raised amyloid PET signal in the hippocampus and thalamus [skb-shokrikojori2018] and slowed clearance of an injected MRI tracer [skb-eide2021]. In 2024, a mouse study using a different method found brain clearance was *reduced* during sleep and anesthesia [skb-miao2024]. Epidemiologically, sleeping 6 hours or less in midlife was associated with about 30% higher dementia risk over 25 years [skb-sabia2021]. Good sleep is worth protecting ([short-sleep], [sleep-apnea]), but "sleep flushes toxins from the brain" is a hypothesis, not established human physiology.

**Hearing.** Hearing loss is one of the largest modifiable risk factors in the Lancet Commission [skb-livingston2024]. The ACHIEVE trial in 977 older adults found hearing aids did not slow 3-year cognitive decline overall, although a prespecified sensitivity analysis suggested slower decline in the older, higher-risk participants recruited from an existing heart-health cohort [skb-lin2023]. Hearing aids are worth using for their own sake; their effect on dementia is unproven.

**Air pollution.** Fine particles and combustion products reach the brain and drive microglial activation and ROS [skb-block2009], and combustion-derived magnetite nanoparticles, a redox-active iron source, have been found in human brain tissue [skb-maher2016]. A BMJ meta-analysis found an overall dementia hazard ratio of 1.04 per 2 ug/m3 PM2.5 (confidence interval crossing 1), rising to 1.42 in studies that actively screened participants for dementia [skb-wilker2023]. The Lancet Commission attributes about 3% of dementia to air pollution [skb-livingston2024]. See [outdoor-air-pollution] and [hepa-air-purifier].

**Alcohol.** In 36,678 UK Biobank adults, alcohol was associated with smaller brain volumes and poorer white matter already at 1-2 units a day [skb-daviet2022]. More than 7 units a week was associated with higher iron in the basal ganglia, and higher iron with poorer cognition, an oxidative link [skb-topiwala2022]. In Whitehall II, 14-21 units a week carried 3.4 times the odds of hippocampal atrophy, and light drinking showed no protection [skb-topiwala2017]. Observational dementia data are U-shaped, with higher risk in both abstainers and those drinking over 14 units a week [skb-sabia2018]. Genetic (Mendelian randomization) analysis in about 560,000 people instead shows risk rising steadily with intake, suggesting the apparent benefit of light drinking reflects people cutting down as cognition starts to slip [skb-topiwala2026]. See [alcohol].

### 2.6 The Lancet Commission frame

The 2024 Lancet Commission lists 14 potentially modifiable risk factors across life: less education, hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, traumatic brain injury, physical inactivity, diabetes, smoking, hypertension, obesity, excessive alcohol, social isolation, air pollution and untreated vision loss. Together they account for about 45% of dementia [skb-livingston2024]. Notice what is not on the list: antioxidant supplements, specific "superfoods" and named diets. These population attributable fractions assume causality from observational data, so they are estimates of potential, not promises; ACHIEVE shows how a top-ranked factor can fail a direct trial [skb-lin2023].

## Sperm and egg relevance

- **No skin-specific intervention here has direct fertility evidence.** Sunscreen, topical vitamin C and topical niacinamide act locally and have no known reproductive effects.
- **Topical retinoids and pregnancy:** oral retinoids are potent teratogens. A prospective cohort found no excess birth defects after first-trimester topical tretinoin [skb-loureiro2005], but stopping topical retinoids when trying to conceive remains the conservative choice.
- **Nicotinamide** is an NAD+ precursor, the same biology discussed for egg aging under [nmn-nad]. The skin-cancer trials provide safety data at 1 g/day for a year in adults, but there are no fertility data.
- **B vitamins:** folate is essential preconception care for its own reasons ([folic-acid]); the cognition doses (folic acid 0.8 mg) overlap the fertility range.
- **Omega-3:** fertility evidence is covered under [omega3-fish]; the null cognition trials do not bear on it.
- **Shared exposures:** smoking, heavy drinking, air pollution and poor sleep harm skin, brain, sperm and eggs alike ([tobacco-smoking], [alcohol], [outdoor-air-pollution], [short-sleep]).

## Where the evidence is contested

1. **Does sunscreen prevent melanoma?** For: the Nambour RCT follow-up (HR 0.50, invasive HR 0.27) [skb-green2011] and a bias-aware meta-analysis showing confounding hides protection in observational data [skb-rueegg2019]. Against: a meta-analysis of 29 mostly case-control studies found no association (OR 1.10) [skb-silva2018], and the RCT result rests on 33 melanomas with P=0.051. Safety: organic filters are systemically absorbed [skb-matta2020], but no harm is shown. Vitamin D: the reassuring consensus [skb-passeron2019] included industry scientists.
2. **How "oxidative" is UVA damage?** CPDs, not 8-oxo-dG, predominate after UVA in whole skin [skb-mouret2006], which weakens the case for topical antioxidants as primary protection.
3. **Visible and infrared light.** Industry-linked lab work shows visible-light ROS and IR-A MMP induction [skb-liebel2012, skb-schroeder2008, skb-gretherbeck2015]; an independent appraisal argues solar IR doses are too low to matter [skb-diffey2016]. Visible light does matter in melasma [skb-castanedo2014].
4. **Topical vitamin C.** Positive trials are small and often developer- or manufacturer-run [skb-lin2005, skb-dedormael2019]; the independent review counted only 139 participants [skb-correia2023].
5. **Nicotinamide.** ONTRAC and a meta-analysis are positive [skb-chen2015, skb-mainville2022]; the transplant trial is null [skb-allen2023]; the large VA cohort is supportive but observational [skb-breglio2025].
6. **Retinoids and mortality.** The VATTC death excess [skb-weinstock2009] alongside no cancer prevention [skb-weinstock2012] is unexplained, though judged non-causal by the investigators.
7. **Vitamin E in Alzheimer disease.** TEAM-AD found slower functional decline [skb-dysken2014] and the 1997 trial was positive only after adjustment [skb-sano1997]; prevention trials were null [skb-petersen2005, skb-kryscio2017], Cochrane found no cognitive benefit [skb-farina2017], and an antioxidant combination worsened cognition [skb-galasko2012].
8. **CoQ10 in Parkinson's.** The positive pilot [skb-shults2002] versus the null phase 3 QE3 [skb-beal2014], a textbook example of a small positive trial failing at scale.
9. **COSMOS multivitamin and cocoa.** Three substudies agree on a small multivitamin benefit [skb-baker2023, skb-yeung2023, skb-vyas2024a], but the effect is about 0.07 SD, the 12-year PHS II trial was null [skb-grodstein2013], and incident MCI did not fall [skb-sachs2023]. For cocoa, the flavanol investigators emphasize the low-intake subgroup [skb-brickman2023]; the primary analyses were null [skb-baker2023, skb-vyas2024b], and Mars, which makes cocoa-flavanol products, supported the trial.
10. **MIND diet.** Cohort benefit [skb-morris2015, skb-hosking2019] versus a null RCT [skb-barnes2023] and a null 20-year cohort [skb-glans2023].
11. **Blueberries.** Positive memory meta-analysis in MCI [skb-dasilva2025] versus null cognition meta-analysis in healthy people [skb-he2026]; trials are tiny [skb-krikorian2010, skb-miller2018].
12. **Omega-3.** Consistent observational association [skb-zhang2016] versus consistently null trials [skb-burckhardt2016, skb-chew2015, skb-quinn2010, skb-andrieu2017, skb-kang2022]; subgroup hypotheses (APOE4, early stage, B-vitamin status) remain untested [skb-yassine2017, skb-jerneren2015].
13. **B vitamins.** VITACOG and the consensus statement [skb-smith2010, skb-smith2018] versus the 22,000-person meta-analysis [skb-clarke2014] and the null Alzheimer trial [skb-aisen2008].
14. **Exercise and the hippocampus.** A 2% volume gain [skb-erickson2011] versus a null total-volume meta-analysis [skb-firth2018], a null cognitive trial [skb-sink2015] and slight harm in dementia [skb-lamb2018].
15. **Sleep and glymphatic clearance.** Supporting mouse and human data [skb-xie2013, skb-shokrikojori2018, skb-eide2021] versus a 2024 mouse study showing reduced clearance during sleep [skb-miao2024].
16. **Hearing aids.** A top Lancet Commission risk factor [skb-livingston2024] versus the overall null ACHIEVE trial, with a possible benefit only in the higher-risk subgroup [skb-lin2023].
17. **Air pollution and dementia.** Plausible mechanisms [skb-block2009, skb-maher2016] versus an overall meta-analytic estimate that crosses 1, with a stronger signal only under active case finding [skb-wilker2023].
18. **Light drinking.** Observational U-shape [skb-sabia2018] versus brain-imaging harm at low intake and genetic evidence of a monotonic risk [skb-daviet2022, skb-topiwala2017, skb-topiwala2026].

## Practical bottom line

**Skin**
1. Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen (SPF 15+) on face, neck, arms and hands, plus shade and clothing at midday: the only proven anti-oxidative-aging step [skb-hughes2013]. Choose a tinted (iron-oxide) product if you have melasma or pigment problems [skb-castanedo2014].
2. If you want a treatment for existing photoaging, use a retinoid (prescription tretinoin or retinol) at night, building up slowly [skb-weiss1988, skb-kafi2007].
3. Topical vitamin C (10-20% L-ascorbic acid, low pH) under sunscreen is an optional extra, mainly for pigment and texture [skb-pinnell2001, skb-correia2023].
4. If you have had skin cancers, ask a dermatologist about nicotinamide 500 mg twice daily [skb-chen2015], knowing it did not help transplant recipients [skb-allen2023].
5. Do not smoke [skb-okada2013]. Skip antioxidant pills for skin [skb-hercberg2007]. Tomato-rich food is fine but is not sun protection [skb-stahl2001].

**Brain**
1. Do not take vitamin E, CoQ10, high-dose antioxidant cocktails or iron chelators to protect your brain; the trials are null or harmful [skb-petersen2005, skb-beal2014, skb-galasko2012, skb-devos2022].
2. Work on the Lancet Commission factors: blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, diabetes, hearing and vision, smoking, heavy drinking, physical activity, social contact, and air-pollution exposure [skb-livingston2024].
3. Exercise regularly and protect your sleep: good for the whole body, plausibly good for the brain, but do not expect measurable memory gains [skb-erickson2011, skb-sink2015, skb-sabia2021].
4. If you are over 60 and want a supplement, a standard multivitamin has the best (still modest) cognitive trial data [skb-vyas2024a]. If you have mild cognitive impairment, have your homocysteine and B12 checked; B vitamins may slow brain atrophy when homocysteine is raised [skb-smith2010].
5. Eat a Mediterranean-type diet with fish, berries, greens and nuts for cardiovascular reasons; do not expect a specific diet to prevent dementia on current trial evidence [skb-barnes2023].
6. Keep alcohol low; there is no brain-protective drinking level [skb-topiwala2017, skb-topiwala2026].

## What we don't know

- Whether topical antioxidants, visible-light or infrared protection change long-term visible aging or skin-cancer rates; no long-term RCTs exist.
- Whether nicotinamide helps beyond 12 months, prevents melanoma, or works in anyone other than high-risk immunocompetent adults.
- Why the tretinoin arm of VATTC had more deaths.
- Whether any antioxidant strategy can help the brain when targeted at the right stage, place or person, for example people with measurably high brain oxidative stress or low baseline intake (the "depletion-repletion" idea behind the COSMOS flavanol subgroup [skb-brickman2023]).
- Whether the modest COSMOS multivitamin benefit translates into less dementia.
- Whether B vitamins prevent dementia in people with high homocysteine and good omega-3 status; a trial designed around these subgroups has not been done.
- How brain clearance actually changes with sleep in humans.
- Whether hearing aids, air-pollution reduction or cutting alcohol lower dementia incidence in randomized or quasi-experimental tests.
