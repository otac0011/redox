# Supplements under audit: what survives when cohorts meet trials

*Audit of the 46 nutrient and supplement factors on Redox Compass, checked against PubMed in October 2026.*

## Why supplements are where cohort findings go to die

Supplements are the area of nutrition where observational studies have most often been overturned by randomized trials. The reason is simple: people who take supplements, and people with high blood levels of a vitamin, are different from those who don't. They tend to be richer, thinner, more active, less likely to smoke, and more likely to eat vegetables and see a doctor. This is the **healthy-user effect**. A cohort study can adjust for the confounders it measured, but not for the ones it missed or measured badly (**residual confounding**).

The second trap is **reverse causation**. Illness, inflammation, obesity and spending less time outdoors all lower blood vitamin levels. So a low level can be a *result* of poor health rather than its cause. A systematic review of 290 cohorts and 172 trials found that strong cohort links between low vitamin D and many diseases were not reproduced in trials, even in people who started below 50 nmol/L, and concluded that low 25(OH)D is mostly a marker of ill health [anut-autier2014].

Three classic reversals set the pattern:

- **Beta-carotene.** High blood beta-carotene tracked lower lung-cancer risk in cohorts, because it marks a vegetable-rich, non-smoking life. In smokers and asbestos workers, 20-30 mg/day pills *raised* lung cancer by 18-28% [anut-atbc1994, anut-omenn1996].
- **Vitamin E.** Supplement users had less heart disease in cohorts. Trials with more than 100,000 people found no benefit, more prostate cancer (HR 1.17) and more haemorrhagic stroke [anut-hope2000, anut-lee2005, anut-klein2011, anut-schurks2010].
- **Vitamin D.** The newest and largest example, covered below.

Newer tools help separate cause from marker. **Mendelian randomization (MR)** uses gene variants that raise a nutrient level from birth as a natural experiment; it avoids lifestyle confounding but has its own traps (pleiotropy, and for non-linear analyses, a stratification method that turned out to be biased). **Target-trial emulation** re-analyses cohort data as if it were a trial, but still rests on observational associations. **Natural experiments** such as the HFE haemochromatosis gene show what lifelong iron loading does.

## The most important reversals and confirmations

**1. Vitamin D and IVF (2021-2026): cohort link not causal.** The site leads with a 2018 meta-analysis showing vitamin D-replete women had about 33% higher odds of IVF live birth [anut-chu2018]. Since then two well-run trials tested the obvious question. SUNDRO (2021) gave 630 women with 25(OH)D below 30 ng/mL a single 600,000 IU dose: clinical pregnancy 37% vs 40% (RR 0.91), with no benefit in any subgroup [anut-somigliana2021]. A 2026 BMJ trial in 24 Chinese centres gave 876 women with PCOS (mean 25(OH)D 16 ng/mL, clearly low) 4000 IU/day for up to 90 days: levels doubled, live birth did not change (52.0% vs 50.2%) [anut-hu2026]. The cohort association was almost certainly confounding by BMI, insulin resistance and overall health.

**2. Vitamin D in general (2019-2024): big trials null, causal genetics corrected.** VITAL (25,871 people) [anut-manson2019], D-Health (21,315) [anut-neale2022] and DO-HEALTH (2,157) [anut-bischoffferrari2020] found no effect on cancer, cardiovascular events, mortality or ageing outcomes. The usual reply is that these people were not deficient. A 2021 non-linear MR paper seemed to show deficiency did causally raise mortality, but it was retracted and republished in 2024: with corrected methods, genetically predicted 25(OH)D had no effect on heart disease, stroke or death at any level, including deficiency [anut-sofianopoulou2021, anut-erfc2024]. Burgess showed why: the residual method assumes the genetic effect on vitamin D is the same at every level, and for 25(OH)D it varies five-fold [anut-burgess2023]. A 2022 Annals paper used the same method [anut-sutherland2022]. The Endocrine Society (2024) now suggests against routine 25(OH)D testing and against doses above the DRI in healthy adults under 75 [anut-demay2024].

**3. Antioxidant vitamins (2014-2022): confirmed by genetics and follow-up.** An MR study of 768,121 people found genetically higher blood retinol, beta-carotene, vitamin C or tocopherols did not lower coronary disease [anut-luo2021]. This confirms the trial results by a separate route. Beta-carotene's excess lung cancer faded after ATBC stopped [anut-virtamo2014] but persisted, non-significantly, after CARET, mostly in women [anut-goodman2004].

**4. Homocysteine and dementia (2017): the causal link fails an MR test.** The site's B-vitamin claim rests on VITACOG, a 168-person MRI trial whose strongest effects are subgroup findings [anut-smith2010]. MR found genetically predicted homocysteine, folate and B12 were not associated with Alzheimer disease [anut-larsson2017], in line with the null 11-trial meta-analysis [anut-clarke2014].

**5. Male fertility supplements (2020-2026): no live-birth benefit.** FAZST (2,370 couples) [anut-schisterman2020] and MOXI [anut-steiner2020] were null. A 2025 meta-analysis of 50 studies found no supplement improved pregnancy or live birth, only single semen parameters at low certainty [anut-michaelsen2025], and a 2026 meta-analysis of 41 RCTs found semen gains with prediction intervals crossing zero [anut-melhem2026]. For astaxanthin, pooled human trials are now null [anut-dehpahni2026].

**6. Omega-3 and atrial fibrillation (2019-2021): a missing harm.** Seven cardiovascular trials (81,210 patients) show marine omega-3 supplements raise atrial fibrillation by 25%, and by 49% above 1 g/day [anut-gencer2021]. REDUCE-IT also showed more AF hospitalisation and borderline more bleeding [anut-bhatt2019]. The site's interactions entry discusses fish-oil bleeding but not AF.

## Where the evidence is contested

**Does vitamin D help people who are truly deficient?** One side: trials enrolled mostly replete people, and 2026 UK Biobank trial emulations predict 15-25% lower mortality and about 25% lower cardiovascular mortality if deficient people raised their levels as much as trial participants did [anut-wang2026a, anut-wang2026b]; a 2022 non-linear MR also reported an L-shaped causal effect [anut-sutherland2022]. Other side: those emulations reuse the observational associations that trials keep failing to confirm; the corrected MR found no effect at any level [anut-erfc2024]; trials in deficient people have been null where they exist (IVF [anut-hu2026, anut-somigliana2021]; Autier's subset below 50 nmol/L [anut-autier2014]). D-Health even showed a numerically higher cancer mortality on vitamin D [anut-neale2022], while its cardiovascular analysis was borderline favourable (HR 0.91) [anut-thompson2023].

**Does vitamin E raise mortality?** Miller 2005 and the Cochrane review say slightly, at high doses [anut-miller2005, anut-bjelakovic2012]. A Bayesian re-analysis [anut-berry2009] and a 57-trial meta-analysis (RR 1.00) [anut-abner2011] say no. Some argue the trials were flawed because doses did not lower oxidative stress [anut-blumberg2007]. All sides agree there is no benefit.

**CoQ10 for egg quality.** A 2026 meta-analysis of three small trials reports higher live birth in poor responders (OR 2.28) [anut-schutz2026]; a 2026 RCT found more oocytes but no pregnancy difference [anut-abdelrahman2026]; ESHRE does not recommend antioxidant add-ons [anut-eshre2023]. For men, one 2025 meta-analysis reports a clinical-pregnancy OR of 6.02 from nine small studies [anut-bakri2025], an effect size that should raise suspicion, against null broader syntheses [anut-michaelsen2025, anut-deligny2022].

**Trustworthiness of small fertility trials.** Mol, Bordewijk and colleagues have shown that PCOS trials that do not share data have more statistical inconsistencies and different pooled effects [anut-bordewijk2020], and flagged one author's 14 RCTs as unreliable [anut-mol2024]. The L-carnitine PCOS trial the site already flags was formally retracted in 2023 [anut-ismail2014, anut-ejogrb2023], and the Safarinejad selenium/NAC trial carries an Expression of Concern [anut-safarinejad2009, anut-jurol2023]. Network meta-analyses built on such trials can rank supplements like curcumin first at very-low certainty [anut-du2025].

**REDUCE-IT's placebo.** The mineral-oil placebo in REDUCE-IT raised LDL, hs-CRP (+22%) and IL-1beta (+29%) in the control arm [anut-ridker2022], so part of the 25% benefit [anut-bhatt2019] may reflect placebo harm; STRENGTH, with corn oil, found nothing [anut-nicholls2020].

**Research integrity in polyphenols.** Curcumin is a chemically unstable compound that interferes with lab assays [anut-nelson2017], and several resveratrol papers from Dipak Das's laboratory were retracted for misconduct [anut-mukherjee2009, anut-dudley2009]. The site cites none of the retracted papers, but its debate fields for curcumin and resveratrol are empty.

**Multivitamins and cognition.** COSMOS-Mind, COSMOS-Web and the clinic substudy all found small benefits [anut-baker2023, anut-yeung2023, anut-vyas2024], but they are one trial programme; PHS II found no cognitive effect [anut-gaziano2012].

## What changed recently (2023-2026)

- 2023: Burgess shows non-linear MR residual method is biased for vitamin D [anut-burgess2023]; Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol expression of concern, then 2024 retraction and null republication [anut-erfc2024].
- 2023-2024: COSMOS cognition sub-studies [anut-baker2023, anut-yeung2023, anut-vyas2024]; 390,000-person cohort finds no mortality benefit of multivitamins [anut-loftfield2024].
- 2024: Endocrine Society vitamin D guideline [anut-demay2024]; 40 mg/day spermidine does not change circulating polyamines [anut-keohane2024].
- 2025: NIH study shows taurine does not decline with age in humans [anut-fernandez2025]; Malmo cohort finds no taurine-dementia link [anut-zhang2025]; male-supplement meta-analysis null for live birth [anut-michaelsen2025]; MitoQ does not alter exercise redox signalling [anut-broome2025].
- 2026: BMJ vitamin D-before-IVF trial null [anut-hu2026]; astaxanthin sperm meta-analysis null [anut-dehpahni2026]; hydrogen water raised MDA in an RCT [anut-zhang2026a]; nicotinamide and Polypodium raise the UVA burn threshold but do not reduce UVA DNA dimers [anut-faisal2026]; NMN gives only a small diastolic BP drop [anut-zhang2026b].

## Practical bottom line

- Do not take beta-carotene, high-dose vitamin E, or antioxidant cocktails for prevention. Trials and genetics agree they do not help, and some harm.
- Correct a known vitamin D deficiency for bone health, but don't expect it to improve fertility, heart health or lifespan. Routine testing in healthy adults is no longer advised by the Endocrine Society.
- Fertility supplements for men have not raised live birth in any well-run trial. For women, CoQ10 in poor responders is the only one with even small-trial live-birth data.
- Gram-dose fish oil raises atrial fibrillation risk. That matters more for most people than the oxidation question.
- Longevity supplements (NMN, NR, spermidine, urolithin A, fisetin, MitoQ, taurine, GlyNAC) raise biomarkers. None has shown a clinical benefit in an independent trial.

## What we still don't know

- Whether vitamin D helps people with severe deficiency (below 25 nmol/L), since no large trial has selected only them.
- Whether CoQ10's egg-side signal survives a large, preregistered, data-sharing trial with live birth as the outcome.
- The long-term safety of NAD precursors, high-dose spermidine and senolytic flavonoids.
