# Egg (oocyte) quality and oxidative stress

Research notes for the "egg" area. Structured data: `data/egg.json` (35 factors, 8 mechanisms, 97 references). Citations are in square brackets and match the reference ids in the JSON. All references were checked against PubMed during this research session (title, PMID and DOI matched). Two trials that are cited were later retracted; they are labelled RETRACTED and are cited only as a warning.

## Bottom line

- **Age is the dominant factor, and you cannot change it.** Female fecundity starts falling at about 32 and falls faster after 37 [acog2014]. In IVF embryos, the chance of having no chromosomally normal embryo at all is 2-6% at ages 26-37, 33% at 42 and 53% at 44 [franasiak2014]. The main cause is chromosome mis-segregation in eggs as the "glue" holding chromosomes together (cohesin) wears out over decades [gruhn2019]. No diet, exercise plan or supplement has been shown to reverse this in humans.
- **The controllable factors are real but smaller.** The strongest are: not smoking [augood1998, waylen2009, zhu2018], not being obese [sermondade2019], taking folic acid [uspstf2023, gaskins2014], keeping alcohol low [rossi2011, fan2017], and eating a Mediterranean-style diet [karayiannis2018, gaskins2019diet].
- **Antioxidant supplements are not a proven fix.** The Cochrane review of 63 trials found only low- to very-low-quality evidence for women [showell2020], and ESHRE's 2023 guidance says antioxidant therapy "is not recommended in ART" [eshre2023addons]. CoQ10 is the most promising, with some benefit on egg numbers and embryo quality in poor responders, but no proven gain in live births [xu2018, florou2020].
- **The window is about 3 months.** The egg you ovulate has been growing in its follicle for roughly the last 3 months (about 70-85 days of the final growth phase) [gougeon1986, gougeon2010]. That is the period lifestyle changes can influence.

## 1. Biology

### Oxidative stress in the ovary is not simply "bad"

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are by-products of metabolism and also signalling molecules. Oxidative stress means ROS outrun antioxidant defences. In the ovary, it has been linked to PCOS, endometriosis, unexplained infertility and pregnancy complications, but the evidence that antioxidants fix it in women is weak and largely from animal or lab studies [agarwal2012].

Some ROS are required. After the LH surge, a burst of ROS drives the events of ovulation: cumulus expansion, progesterone production and EGF-receptor signalling. When mice were given antioxidants directly into the ovary, fewer eggs were ovulated, and hydrogen peroxide alone could mimic LH [shkolnik2011]. This is a clear example of why "more antioxidant" is not automatically better.

### Folliculogenesis timeline: why 3 months matters

Human follicle growth is slow. A resting follicle needs several months to reach the preantral stage, then about 70 more days to reach 2 mm [gougeon2010]. The follicle that ovulates in a given cycle came from a cohort that started its final growth phase about 85 days earlier and was selected 15-20 days before ovulation [gougeon1986]. So the egg released next month is already well along; changes made today mostly affect eggs ovulated 2-3 months from now. This is the biological basis for a 3-month preparation plan.

### Mitochondria and mitochondrial DNA

The egg is the most mitochondria-rich cell in the body and depends on its mitochondria for the energy needed for maturation, fertilization and early embryo development [maypanloup2016]. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is passed on only by the mother and passes through a "bottleneck" in the germline, so the proportion of any faulty mtDNA can shift sharply between generations [cree2008]. Because mtDNA comes only from the egg and does not mix with paternal DNA, the egg is responsible for the stable transmission of the mitochondrial genome across generations [maypanloup2016].

With age, eggs and their supporting granulosa cells show less efficient mitochondria, more mtDNA instability and impaired mitochondrial biogenesis [maypanloup2016]. In mice and women, older eggs make less of the enzymes that synthesise coenzyme Q10; in mice, CoQ10 restored mitochondrial function and fertility [benmeir2015]. Ageing mouse eggs also lose NAD+, and the precursor NMN restored egg quality in aged mice [bertoldo2020, miao2020]. These are animal findings; human proof is missing.

### Age, cohesin and aneuploidy

Eggs pause partway through meiosis from before birth until ovulation, which can be 40+ years. Chromosome errors (aneuploidy) in eggs follow a U-shaped curve: slightly higher in the very young, lowest in the late 20s, and rising steeply after the mid-30s. In older women the main driver is loss of cohesion at and around centromeres [gruhn2019]. In 15,169 embryo biopsies, aneuploidy was lowest between 26 and 30 and rose predictably after that; the risk of having no euploid embryo was 33% at 42 and 53% at 44 [franasiak2014]. Aneuploid embryos usually fail to implant or miscarry, which is why age drives both infertility and miscarriage.

The honest implication: cohesin loss is a mechanical, time-dependent process. Oxidative stress may add to it, but there is no human evidence that any antioxidant prevents it.

### Follicular fluid oxidative markers and IVF outcomes

Higher ROS and lipid peroxidation in follicular fluid were associated with fertilization failure and poor embryos in 78 IVF patients [das2006]. Higher follicular 8-OHdG (a marker of oxidative DNA damage) was found where many eggs degenerated [tamura2008]. Obese women have more insulin, triglycerides, lactate and C-reactive protein in their follicular fluid [robker2009]. These are research markers, not validated clinical tests, and they are not routinely measured.

### The egg repairs sperm DNA damage

Until the embryo's own genes switch on at the 4-8 cell stage, DNA repair relies entirely on repair machinery stored in the egg [menezo2010]. Egg quality therefore affects how well the embryo copes with sperm DNA damage. In one cohort, sperm DNA fragmentation reduced pregnancy when the patient's own eggs were used, but had no significant effect with donor eggs from young women [meseguer2011]. This is one way female age and male DNA damage interact.

## 2. Conditions with elevated oxidative stress

**PCOS.** A meta-analysis of 68 studies found women with PCOS have 47% higher malondialdehyde (lipid peroxidation), 23% higher homocysteine and about 50% lower glutathione than controls, even after matching for weight [murri2013]. Most PCOS infertility is anovulation, which is treatable. The 2023 international guideline puts lifestyle first, then evidence-based medical and fertility treatment [teede2023]. IVF live birth is worse when obesity and PCOS combine [sermondade2019]. In an early study, women with PCOS who lost more than 5% of body weight had lower insulin, higher SHBG and better cycles; those who lost less did not [kiddy1992]. A Cochrane review found lifestyle trials in PCOS have not yet reported live birth [lim2019].

**Endometriosis.** Eggs from women with endometriosis more often fail to mature in the lab, look abnormal and have fewer mitochondria, and granulosa cells generate more ROS [sanchez2017]. Clinically, women with endometrioma have fewer eggs retrieved and more cancelled IVF cycles, but similar live birth rates once they reach transfer (OR 0.98), and surgery before IVF did not improve outcomes [hamdan2015].

**Obesity.** Obesity (BMI 30 or more) is associated with about 15% lower IVF live birth (RR 0.85) [sermondade2019]. In obese women the follicle is more inflamed and lipid-rich [robker2009]; in mice a high-fat diet loads eggs with fat, damages mitochondria and causes ER stress ("lipotoxicity") [wu2010].

**Diabetes and insulin resistance.** Women with type 1 diabetes had 24% lower, and type 2 diabetes 36% lower, fecundability, even with regular cycles [whitworth2011]. In diabetic mice, eggs have abnormal mitochondria, less ATP, more spindle defects and more aneuploidy [wang2009].

**Age** is covered above. It is the factor that dwarfs all others.

**Smoking** is the largest avoidable toxin. Smokers have 60% higher odds of infertility and about a third lower odds of IVF pregnancy per cycle [augood1998]. In ART, the odds of live birth are roughly halved (OR 0.54) and miscarriage more than doubles (OR 2.65) [waylen2009]. Smokers have lower AMH and fewer mature eggs [freour2008]. Across 207,231 women, current smokers had about twice the risk of premature menopause, rising with years smoked; women who had quit more than 10 years earlier had the same risk as never-smokers [zhu2018]. Smoke contains thousands of toxicants, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and cadmium, that act on follicle development [dechanet2011]. ASRM's 2024 opinion also reviews nicotine and marijuana [asrm2024tobacco].

**Obesity** is covered under conditions. Weight loss is discussed under helpful factors, with an important nuance.

**Alcohol.** Data conflict. A meta-analysis of 19 studies found lower fecundability with any drinking (RR 0.87), and more so above 12.5 g ethanol a day, about one drink (RR 0.77) [fan2017]. A Danish cohort of 6,120 women found no detectable effect below 14 drinks a week [mikkelsen2016]. In 2,545 IVF couples, women who drank 4 or more drinks a week had 16% lower odds of live birth [rossi2011], while the EARTH IVF cohort found no effect up to about 12 g a day [abadia2017]. ASRM advises avoiding more than 2 drinks a day when trying [asrm2022natural]. A sensible line: none or very little while trying, and under 4 drinks a week at most before IVF.

**Caffeine: keep fertility and miscarriage separate.** Coffee or caffeine shows little or no link to how quickly women conceive or to IVF success [lyngso2017, abadia2017, wesselink2016]. The concern is miscarriage once pregnant: risk rises about 7% per 100 mg a day, with RR 1.40 at 350-699 mg and 1.72 at 700 mg or more [chen2016]. In a Danish cohort, caffeine before conception was not linked to miscarriage, but intake in early pregnancy was [hahn2015]. ASRM: more than 500 mg a day has been linked to lower fertility, while 1-2 cups a day shows no apparent harm [asrm2022natural]. Practical limit: under 200 mg a day (1-2 cups of brewed coffee) while trying and in pregnancy.

**Air pollution.** In 7,403 women having IVF, higher NO2 at home and at the lab was linked to lower live birth [legro2010]. Higher NO2 was also linked to lower AMH [lamarca2020]. In another IVF cohort, the NO2 association was seen only in women taking less than 800 ug a day of supplemental folate [gaskins2019air], an intriguing but unconfirmed finding.

**Endocrine disruptors (EARTH study).** In the EARTH cohort run by Hauser, Gaskins and colleagues, women in the highest quartile of urinary DEHP phthalate metabolites had fewer eggs and 19 percentage points lower live birth than the lowest quartile [hauser2016]. Each log-unit increase in urinary BPA was linked to 12% fewer eggs [moklin2010]. These are single, modest-sized cohorts with noisy exposure measures.

**Heavy metals.** In 56 IVF patients, higher follicular-fluid mercury and lead were linked to lower live birth [butts2021]. Fish is the main mercury source, but it is also the main omega-3 source, so choose smaller, lower-mercury fish rather than avoiding fish.

**Chemotherapy and radiation.** Gonadotoxic chemotherapy breaks DNA in resting eggs and triggers their death within 12-24 hours [szymanska2020]. Under 2 Gy of radiation kills half of human eggs [wallace2003]. ASCO advises discussing fertility preservation before treatment; egg and embryo freezing are standard care [oktay2018].

**Very intense exercise and energy deficiency.** In 3,628 Danish women, 5 or more hours a week of vigorous exercise was linked to 32% lower monthly chance of conception, but not in women with BMI 25 or more [wise2012]. In Norway, women who exercised on most days had 3.2 times the odds of fertility problems, and those exercising to exhaustion 2.3 times [gudmundsdottir2009]. Low energy availability (RED-S) causes reproductive dysfunction through hypothalamic suppression [mountjoy2023]. This is an energy-signalling problem, not mainly an oxidative one. Vigorous exercise was not linked to worse IVF outcomes in the EARTH cohort [gaskins2016pa].

**Sleep and circadian disruption.** In 6,873 women, trouble sleeping more than half the time was linked to 13% lower fecundability [willis2019]. Shift work was linked to irregular cycles, and night shifts to early miscarriage (aOR 1.41), but the link with infertility did not survive adjustment [stocker2014].

**Psychological stress.** One cohort linked a stress biomarker (salivary alpha-amylase) to 29% lower fecundability [lynch2014]. But a meta-analysis of 3,583 IVF patients found that anxiety or depression before treatment did not affect pregnancy rates [boivin2011]. Stress management is good for wellbeing; it should not be sold as a fertility treatment, and people should not be blamed for being stressed.

## 4. Factors that may help

**Mediterranean diet.** In 244 non-obese Greek women having their first IVF, those in the lowest third of Mediterranean-diet adherence had a live birth rate of 26.6% vs 48.8% in the highest third (adjusted RR 0.32), but only in women under 35, and with no effect on egg or embryo measures [karayiannis2018]. A Dutch cohort found couples with high adherence had higher pregnancy odds (OR 1.4) and higher folate and vitamin B6 in follicular fluid [vujkovic2010]. In the US EARTH cohort, the benefit appeared once women moved out of the lowest quartile, with no extra gain at higher adherence [gaskins2019diet]. A 2023 meta-analysis judged the pooled evidence too inconsistent to support clinical recommendations [muffone2023]. No randomised trial has tested it. Verdict: good for general health, plausibly helpful, unproven for eggs.

**The "profertility diet".** Gaskins and colleagues built a score from factors linked to IVF success in their cohort: supplemental folic acid, B12 and vitamin D, low-pesticide produce, whole grains, dairy, soy and seafood. Each SD of adherence was linked to 53% higher odds of live birth, and the top vs bottom quartile to 28 percentage points more live births [gaskins2019diet]. It was not linked to egg numbers, which suggests effects beyond the egg. Because it was built and tested in the same 357 women, it needs replication. An earlier "fertility diet" from the Nurses' Health Study II was linked to 66% lower risk of ovulatory infertility [chavarro2007].

**Folic acid.** Everyone who could become pregnant should take 400-800 ug a day to prevent neural tube defects [uspstf2023]; ASRM says at least 400 ug [asrm2022natural]. It is also consistently linked to better fertility [gaskins2018]: 15% higher fecundability in users [cueto2016] and 20 percentage points higher IVF live birth with more than 800 ug a day of supplemental folate vs less than 400 ug [gaskins2014].

**Vitamin D.** Vitamin D-replete women had 33% higher odds of IVF live birth than deficient women in a meta-analysis of cohorts [chu2018]. But in 5 RCTs in deficient women, supplementation improved only chemical pregnancy, not clinical pregnancy [zhou2022]. Check levels and correct deficiency; do not expect more than that.

**Omega-3 fats.** In 100 IVF patients, each 1% higher serum long-chain omega-3 was linked to 8% higher probability of live birth [chiu2018]. Mice fed omega-3-rich diets kept better egg quality into older age [nehra2012]. No adequate human RCT exists.

**Weight loss when obese: the LIFEstyle nuance.** In overweight and obese women, diet-plus-exercise programmes increased pregnancy (RR 1.59) and ovulation [best2017]. But two well-run RCTs found that delaying fertility treatment for weight loss did not improve births from treatment. In the Dutch LIFEstyle trial (577 women, BMI 29 or more), a 6-month lifestyle program (mean loss 4.4 kg) led to 27.1% healthy term singleton births vs 35.2% with prompt treatment [mutsaerts2016]. In a Nordic trial (BMI 30-35), losing an average of 9.4 kg on a 12-week low-calorie diet did not change IVF live birth (29.6% vs 27.5%), although more women conceived naturally (10.5% vs 2.6%) [einarsson2017]. Reading: weight loss helps natural conception and pregnancy health, but for women already headed to IVF, especially over 35, long delays cost time and egg quality.

**Moderate exercise.** In the Danish cohort, 5 or more hours a week of moderate activity was linked to slightly higher fecundability (FR 1.18) [wise2012]. In women with reproductive problems, exercise interventions roughly doubled pregnancy and live birth vs no therapy in a small set of trials [mena2019].

**Stopping smoking** is the single most effective controllable step (see above) [zhu2018].

## 5. Supplements, done carefully

**The big picture.** The Cochrane review "Antioxidants for female subfertility" pooled 63 trials of 7,760 women covering NAC, melatonin, L-arginine, myo-inositol, carnitine, selenium, vitamins C, D, E and B, CoQ10 and omega-3. It found an apparent rise in live birth (OR 1.81), but rated the evidence very low quality, and concluded there is limited evidence for antioxidants in subfertile women [showell2020]. Since publication, 7 of its included trials have been retracted and 2 more have expressions of concern; Cochrane says removing them does not change the conclusions [showell2020]. ESHRE's 2023 add-ons guidance: antioxidant therapy "lacks substantial and reliable evidence" for higher live birth and "is not recommended in ART" [eshre2023addons]. ESHRE's stimulation guideline also does not recommend adjuvants such as DHEA, growth hormone or metformin to improve IVF efficacy [eshre2020stim].

**CoQ10.** The best-supported option. In an RCT of 186 women under 35 with low ovarian reserve, 600 mg a day (200 mg three times a day) for 60 days before IVF increased eggs retrieved (median 4 vs 2), fertilization and top-quality embryos, and fewer transfers were cancelled. Cumulative live birth was 29% vs 16%, which was not statistically significant [xu2018]. A meta-analysis of 5 RCTs found higher clinical pregnancy (OR 2.44) but no significant live-birth effect (OR 1.67) [florou2020]. A network meta-analysis in poor responders found higher clinical pregnancy with CoQ10 (OR 2.22) and the lowest cancellation rate [zhang2020]. Mechanism is plausible from mice [benmeir2015]. Caveats: small, open-label, no placebo. Reasonable for poor responders who want to try something low-risk; not proven.

**Melatonin.** Pooled IVF RCTs: higher clinical pregnancy (OR 1.43) and more eggs and good embryos, but not live birth (OR 1.38, not significant); all low quality [hu2020]. The early supportive studies used 3 mg a day and were not randomised [tamura2008]. A placebo-controlled trial of 2, 4 or 8 mg twice daily found no change in follicle numbers [fernando2020], and these doses did not cause daytime sleepiness [fernando2017]. Cochrane found no difference between lower and higher doses [showell2020]. Verdict: mixed, unproven.

**Myo-inositol in PCOS.** The Cochrane review of 13 trials (1,472 women) rated evidence very low and could not show a live-birth benefit (OR 2.42, 95% CI 0.75-7.83) [showell2018]. The review for the 2023 PCOS guideline called the evidence limited and inconclusive, with possible metabolic benefits [fitz2024, teede2023]. A large, widely cited trial of myo-inositol 4 g plus melatonin 3 mg was retracted in 2026 [pacchiarotti2016]. Verdict: low-risk, uncertain benefit, only relevant in PCOS.

**NAC in PCOS.** A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs found better ovulation and pregnancy vs placebo but worse than metformin, with high risk of bias [thakker2015]. The landmark trial used 1.2 g a day with clomiphene and reported 49.3% vs 1.3% ovulation [rizk2005], an implausibly large contrast that warrants caution.

**DHEA in poor responders.** The 2024 Cochrane review (28 RCTs) found DHEA probably makes little or no difference to live birth (OR 1.30, moderate certainty), whereas testosterone pre-treatment probably improves it (OR 2.53) [naik2024]. An early small trial of 75 mg a day reported 23% vs 4% live birth [wiser2010], a typical example of small trials overstating benefit. ESHRE does not recommend DHEA [eshre2020stim, eshre2023addons].

**Vitamins E and C.** Vitamin E 600 mg a day lowered follicular oxidative markers in a tiny study [tamura2008]. Live-birth evidence is lacking [showell2020]. Antioxidant supplements do not appear to help women in fertility treatment [gaskins2018].

**L-carnitine.** The most cited positive trial in women (3 g a day in clomiphene-resistant PCOS) has been retracted [ismail2014]. What remains is weak [showell2020].

**Resveratrol: possible harm.** Resveratrol may support ovarian function through SIRT1, but it suppresses decidualization, the endometrial change needed for implantation, and reviewers advise avoiding it in the luteal phase and pregnancy [ochiai2020]. In a retrospective study, women taking 200 mg a day during embryo-transfer cycles had lower clinical pregnancy (OR 0.54) and more miscarriage (OR 2.60) [ochiai2019]. A small RCT of 800 mg a day in PCOS improved follicular oxidative markers and egg maturity, but not pregnancy rates [ardehjani2024]. Verdict: avoid while trying and around transfer.

**NMN and NAD+ precursors.** Restored egg quality and fertility in aged mice [bertoldo2020, miao2020]. There are no human data on egg quality or pregnancy. Marketing that promises "egg rejuvenation" is extrapolating from mice.

**Alpha-lipoic acid.** A systematic review reports some positive effects on egg maturation, embryo grade and endometriosis pain, but trials are small and usually combined with other products [ditucci2021].

**Evidence-based bottom line on supplements.**
1. Take folic acid (400-800 ug a day). This is standard care [uspstf2023].
2. Correct vitamin D deficiency if tested low [chu2018, zhou2022].
3. If you are a poor responder going into IVF, CoQ10 600 mg a day for about 2 months is a reasonable, low-risk option to discuss with your clinic, knowing live-birth benefit is unproven [xu2018, florou2020].
4. If you have PCOS, myo-inositol is low-risk but of uncertain benefit [showell2018, fitz2024].
5. Skip DHEA unless your specialist recommends it [naik2024], avoid resveratrol around conception and transfer [ochiai2019], and do not buy NMN for fertility [bertoldo2020].
6. Do not use multi-ingredient "egg quality" stacks as a substitute for not smoking, a healthy weight and time.

## 6. What moves the needle most for egg quality

This ranking weighs effect size against strength of evidence. It applies to women trying to conceive or preparing for IVF.

**Not modifiable, and the dominant factor: age.** Aneuploidy rises steeply after the mid-30s and no intervention reverses it in humans [franasiak2014, gruhn2019]. The modifiable levers below can avoid adding damage on top of age; they cannot buy back years. The most important "age" decisions are about timing: seek evaluation after 6 months of trying from 35 and immediately over 40 [acog2014], and, for those planning to delay pregnancy, egg freezing earlier rather than later.

Ranked modifiable factors:

1. **Do not smoke (and quit at least 3 months before trying or IVF).** Large, consistent, dose-dependent harm: about halved IVF live birth odds, lower AMH and earlier menopause [waylen2009, freour2008, zhu2018]. High confidence.
2. **Avoid obesity; aim for BMI under 30 and ideally 18.5-24.9.** About 15% lower IVF live birth with obesity [sermondade2019] and clear follicular-environment changes [robker2009]. Weight loss increases natural pregnancy [best2017], but RCTs show it does not improve IVF live birth, so do not delay treatment for long, especially over 35 [mutsaerts2016, einarsson2017]. Also avoid the opposite extreme: underweight and low energy availability [mountjoy2023].
3. **Take folic acid 400-800 ug a day.** Proven for neural tube defects and consistently linked to better fertility and IVF success [uspstf2023, cueto2016, gaskins2014]. Cheap, safe, high value.
4. **Manage medical conditions:** PCOS, diabetes and endometriosis with your doctor [teede2023, whitworth2011, hamdan2015]. Effects are moderate to large for affected women.
5. **Keep alcohol low:** ideally none while trying, and under 4 drinks a week before IVF [rossi2011, fan2017]. Moderate confidence; data conflict at low intakes [mikkelsen2016].
6. **Eat a Mediterranean-style or "profertility" diet**, including regular lower-mercury fish [karayiannis2018, gaskins2019diet, chiu2018]. Effects in cohorts are large, but evidence is observational and inconsistent [muffone2023].
7. **Move moderately; if lean, cap vigorous exercise below about 5 hours a week** [wise2012, gudmundsdottir2009]. Moderate confidence.
8. **Caffeine under 200 mg a day.** Mainly for miscarriage risk once pregnant, not egg quality [chen2016, lyngso2017].
9. **Reduce endocrine disruptors and pollution where practical** (less packaged food, no heating in plastic, avoid traffic exposure) [hauser2016, moklin2010, legro2010]. Low confidence, low cost.
10. **Sleep well and manage stress** for wellbeing; effects on fertility are small or unproven [willis2019, boivin2011].
11. **Supplements beyond folic acid:** CoQ10 in poor responders is the only one with a reasonable case, and its live-birth benefit is unproven [xu2018, florou2020]. General antioxidant supplements are not recommended for IVF [eshre2023addons, showell2020].

Honest uncertainty: apart from smoking and obesity, most of the evidence is observational, from fertility-clinic cohorts, and often from the same research groups. Healthier people tend to do many healthy things at once, so the separate effect of each item is uncertain. The ranking above is a best judgement, not a precise ordering.

## 7. Timeline: what to do in the 3 months before trying to conceive or before IVF

The egg ovulated or retrieved has been growing for roughly 3 months [gougeon1986, gougeon2010]. The plan below covers that window. If time is short (age over 35, or IVF already scheduled), do not delay treatment to complete it; start everything at once.

**About 3 months before (month 1)**
- Stop smoking completely; get cessation support [asrm2024tobacco, zhu2018].
- Start folic acid 400-800 ug a day, usually as a prenatal vitamin [uspstf2023].
- See a doctor: review medications, check vitamin D, glucose/HbA1c if at risk, and get PCOS, diabetes or endometriosis under management [teede2023, chu2018].
- If BMI is 30 or more, start a reduced-calorie diet plus exercise; a loss of 5% or more of body weight is a realistic first target, but do not let it delay IVF for months if you are over 35 [best2017, kiddy1992, einarsson2017].
- Cut alcohol to none, or well under 4 drinks a week [rossi2011].
- Bring caffeine under 200 mg a day [chen2016].
- Shift toward a Mediterranean-style diet: vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, olive oil, nuts, and lower-mercury fish [karayiannis2018, gaskins2019diet, butts2021].
- If lean and training hard, cut vigorous exercise below about 5 hours a week and make sure you eat enough to keep regular cycles [wise2012, mountjoy2023].
- If heading to IVF as a poor responder, discuss CoQ10 600 mg a day with your clinic now (the RCT used 60 days before stimulation) [xu2018].
- Stop resveratrol and NMN products [ochiai2019, bertoldo2020].

**About 2 months before (month 2)**
- Keep all of the above going; consistency over the whole window matters more than perfection.
- Reduce easy endocrine-disruptor exposures: fewer canned and packaged foods, do not heat food in plastic [hauser2016, moklin2010].
- Build a regular sleep schedule; treat insomnia or sleep apnea [willis2019].
- Include moderate activity most days [wise2012, mena2019].

**The final month (month 3) and during IVF or the conception cycle**
- Continue folic acid, no smoking, minimal or no alcohol, caffeine under 200 mg a day.
- Avoid starting new, untested supplements right before or during stimulation (ESHRE does not recommend antioxidant add-ons in IVF) [eshre2023addons]; tell your clinic everything you take.
- Avoid resveratrol in the luteal phase and around embryo transfer [ochiai2020].
- Stress management for wellbeing; your stress level is not going to ruin the cycle [boivin2011].

## 9. Research-integrity notes

- Fertility supplement research has a retraction problem. Seven trials in the Cochrane antioxidant review have been retracted [showell2020], as has the main L-carnitine trial in women [ismail2014] and a large myo-inositol plus melatonin trial [pacchiarotti2016]. Implausibly large effects from single centres should be treated with suspicion.
- Many apparent "egg quality" benefits are on surrogate outcomes (eggs retrieved, embryo grade, clinical pregnancy) that do not reliably translate into live births [florou2020, hu2020].
- Several diet findings come from one Boston cohort (EARTH) [gaskins2019diet, gaskins2014, chiu2018, hauser2016]. They are well conducted but need independent replication.
- Follicular-fluid oxidative markers are research tools [das2006]; there is no validated clinical test of "egg oxidative stress".
