# Timing for the body: exercise, fasting, sleep, stress and metabolism

The owner asked: **are some effects slow to make a difference and then grow over time, like fitness?** Yes. Most of the factors in this group work on four clocks at once, and it helps to keep them apart:

1. **Signal clock (hours).** A workout, a sugary drink, a late meal or a bright evening light changes redox signalling, glucose or melatonin within minutes to hours, and the change is gone within a day or two.
2. **Protein-turnover clock (days to weeks).** Repeating the signal builds new proteins: mitochondrial enzymes, antioxidant enzymes, glucose transporters. These plateau in weeks and are lost again in weeks once the signal stops.
3. **Tissue clock (weeks to months).** Heart size, blood volume, muscle size, fat mass and HbA1c change over months.
4. **Long-lived-damage and risk clock (years).** Glycation of collagen, coronary risk from shift work, and the survival advantage of fitness build and fade over years.

Fitness is the clearest "slow-build" example: the signal fires after every session, the enzymes follow within a week or two, VO2max climbs for months, and the survival benefit tracks fitness held for years. But fitness is also lost faster than it is gained, so it behaves like a bank balance that needs regular deposits.

## Exercise: from a two-hour burst to a ten-year benefit

**Hours.** Hard exercise raises blood oxidative markers that peak between about 1 and 4 hours afterwards (lipid peroxidation at ~1.2 h, protein carbonyls at ~4.4 h, the glutathione ratio dips at ~1.7 h) and settle within 24 h [tbody-michailidis2007]. Even a 50 km ultramarathon nearly doubled F2-isoprostanes, which were back to baseline the next day [tbody-mastaloudis2001]. That burst is the signal: PGC-1alpha mRNA jumps more than 10-fold 4 h after an interval session and is back to baseline by 24 h, a saw-tooth pattern that repeats with each session [tbody-perry2010], and Nrf2 and HO-1 rise after a single bout [tbody-islam2020].

**One to two days.** One session raises muscle glucose uptake and insulin sensitivity, still measurable 48 h later [tbody-mikines1988], but the effect is "short-lived and disappears after approximately 48 h" in reviews [tbody-hawley2008]. In trained men, 5 days off training brought insulin sensitivity down to untrained levels [tbody-mikines1989], and 10 days off doubled the insulin response to a glucose drink without any change in VO2max [tbody-heath1983]. A single bout needs to be large (about 900 kcal) to show next-morning effects on its own [tbody-magkos2008]. In older women, the improvement measured 3-5 days after the last session had already gone, while younger women kept it [tbody-goulet2005]. This is the main reason exercise needs to be **frequent**: much of its metabolic benefit is a rolling residue of the last session or two.

**One to six weeks.** Repeated pulses build proteins. In sedentary men cycling daily, mitochondrial markers rose within the first week and VO2peak was up 17.5% after 14 days [tbody-egan2013]. PGC-1alpha protein plateaued after 3 to 7 interval sessions [tbody-perry2010]. Four weeks of Tabata intervals raised citrate synthase and Nrf2 protein, although HO-1 protein fell [tbody-islam2020]. Six weeks raised maximal mitochondrial respiration 40% [tbody-tonkonogi2000].

**Months.** VO2max was higher within 3 weeks and was still climbing at 12 weeks (+18% in young, +31% in older men), with early gains from muscle oxygen extraction and later gains from a bigger heart output [tbody-murias2010]. In older adults, trials average about +3.8 mL/kg/min [tbody-huang2016]; in young and middle-aged adults about +5 mL/kg/min [tbody-milanovic2015]. People who seem not to respond do respond when weekly dose is raised [tbody-montero2017].

**Years.** Men who moved from unfit to fit between two exams about five years apart had 44% lower mortality than men who stayed unfit [tbody-blair1995]; keeping or gaining fitness over six years was associated with 30-39% lower mortality than losing it, about 15% per MET gained [tbody-lee2011]. Over 11 years, every 1 mL/kg/min less decline in VO2max meant 9% lower mortality [tbody-laukkanen2016]. And it is not too late: adults inactive in early life who became active at 40-61 had mortality as low as lifelong exercisers [tbody-saintmaurice2019].

**Detraining.** In long-trained athletes VO2max fell 7% in 3 weeks and stabilised 16% lower by 8 weeks, and muscle oxidative enzymes halved in about 12 days, yet capillaries and enzymes stayed above sedentary levels even after 12 weeks [tbody-coyle1984]. Reviews conclude that short breaks (under 4 weeks) cut blood volume, VO2max and insulin sensitivity quickly [tbody-mujika2000a], and that **recently acquired** VO2max gains are completely lost after more than 4 weeks off [tbody-mujika2000b]. Two weeks of very low step counts cut VO2max 7% and insulin sensitivity 17% in young men [tbody-kroghmadsen2010]. So the longer you have been fit, the more of it you keep; a 3-month programme mostly evaporates within 1-2 months of stopping.

**How much to maintain versus build?** Building needs more than keeping. After 10 weeks of 6-day-a-week training, cutting to 2 or 4 days a week at the same intensity held VO2max for 15 more weeks [tbody-hickson1981], but cutting intensity by a third did not [tbody-hickson1985]. A review puts the endurance maintenance dose at about 2 sessions a week, or a third to two-thirds less volume, as long as intensity is kept, and strength maintenance at 1 session a week with 1 set per exercise [tbody-spiering2021].

**Resistance training.** A strength session keeps muscle protein synthesis raised for 48 h [tbody-phillips1997]. Strength rises in the first 3-5 weeks mainly through the nervous system; after that, muscle growth dominates [tbody-moritani1979], and protein synthesis only tracks growth from about week 3 once muscle damage settles [tbody-damas2016]. After 20 weeks off, muscle size was back to baseline but about 60% of the strength gain remained [tbody-psilander2019]. One-third or one-ninth of the training dose for 32 weeks preserved muscle in young but not in older adults [tbody-bickel2011]. Whether muscle has a "memory" that speeds regrowth is debated (below).

**Antioxidant megadoses.** Because adaptation depends on the ROS pulse, flattening it with gram doses of vitamin C plus E blunted insulin-sensitivity gains within 4 weeks [tbody-ristow2009] and mitochondrial and lean-mass gains over 11-12 weeks [tbody-paulsen2014, tbody-bjornsen2016]. Vitamin C saturates blood at about 1 g/day within days and extra is excreted [tbody-levine1996]; vitamin E took 16 weeks to reach its maximum effect on F2-isoprostanes [tbody-roberts2007]. Does timing around sessions matter? It has not been tested properly. In Bjørnsen's trial the vitamins were taken only just before and after each session and still blunted lean-mass gain [tbody-bjornsen2016]; nobody has compared the same dose taken far from training. The ISSN position stand agrees that timing, dose and type all matter but recommends food over supplements [tbody-gonzalez2026].

**Cold water.** Ten minutes of cold immersion after strength sessions reduced satellite-cell and anabolic responses 24-48 h later and, repeated for 12 weeks, gave smaller gains in strength and muscle [tbody-roberts2015]; 15 min at 10 C for 7 weeks cut fibre growth but not strength [tbody-fyfe2019]. A meta-analysis found harm to strength gains but not to endurance gains [tbody-malta2021]. No trial has tested how many hours apart cold and lifting must be; the 24-48 h signalling window suggests using cold on non-lifting days or well apart (an extrapolation). Separately, 10 days of mild cold acclimation raised insulin sensitivity 43% in type 2 diabetes [tbody-hanssen2015].

**Too much.** Ramping training load over 4 weeks improved mitochondria until the highest-load week, when mitochondrial function and glucose tolerance fell sharply [tbody-flockhart2021]; a 3-week overtraining block raised urinary isoprostanes 7-fold, still 96 h after the last session [tbody-margonis2007]. The single-race spike is harmless and gone within a day; the multi-week overload is the problem.

**Short bursts and walks.** Six 1-minute hard intervals before meals lowered the next 24 h of glucose [tbody-francois2014]; walking after meals lowers that meal's glucose, with longer walks doing more [tbody-kang2023, tbody-reynolds2016]. Three 20-second sprints spread through the day raised fitness over 6 weeks about as much as doing them together [tbody-little2019]. These effects are per meal and per day: they do not store up.

**Sitting.** Two-minute walks every 20 minutes cut post-meal glucose the same day [tbody-dunstan2012]. Short bed rest cut insulin-stimulated glucose disposal by 42% without raising mitochondrial H2O2 [tbody-dirks2020]. Young people recover quickly when they move again [tbody-heath1983], but prediabetic older adults had not recovered glycaemic control two weeks after a two-week inactive spell [tbody-mcglory2018].

## Fasting and eating schedules

**Ketosis onset.** The metabolic switch to fat and ketones starts beyond about 12 h without food [tbody-anton2018], but a 20-h fast raised beta-hydroxybutyrate only to 0.10 mmol/L [tbody-halberg2005]; strict 36-h fasts every other day kept ketones up even on eating days [tbody-stekovic2019]. On a ketogenic diet, elite athletes reached the fat-burning levels of months-long adaptation within 5-6 days, and lost it within 5-6 days of eating carbohydrate again [tbody-burke2021]. LDL rose within 3-4 weeks in lean adults [tbody-retterstol2018, tbody-buren2021].

**Autophagy.** Human data are thin. A 36-h fast changed muscle autophagy markers only modestly, and only in untrained men [tbody-dethlefsen2018]. Four days of early eating windows raised the autophagy gene LC3A in blood cells [tbody-jamshed2019]. Plasma proteome changes during water-only fasting appeared only after about 3 days [tbody-pietzner2024], and a 72-h fast made muscle markedly insulin resistant [tbody-vendelbo2012].

**Build and fade.** Most fasting benefits are those of weight loss: alternate-day fasting and daily 25% restriction lost the same weight at 6 and 12 months [tbody-trepanowski2017], as did 5:2 [tbody-harvie2011, tbody-sundfor2018], and Cochrane found no meaningful difference from continuous restriction [tbody-allaf2021]. Calorie restriction lowered F2-isoprostanes at 12 months and kept them down at 24 [tbody-ilyasova2018]. Two years after CALERIE ended, people had regained 54% of the lost weight but were still lighter than at baseline [tbody-marlatt2017]; across structured weight-loss programmes, only 17-29% of lost weight remained after 4-5 years [tbody-anderson2001]. Time-restricted eating gave about 3% weight loss in 8 weeks [tbody-cienfuegos2020] but nothing extra over calorie restriction at 12 months [tbody-liu2022].

**Fasting-mimicking diet.** Three monthly 5-day cycles lowered weight, IGF-1 and blood pressure, and in the full text these changes persisted about 3 months after the last cycle in the 50 people followed up [tbody-wei2017]. That is the main "lasting" claim in this group, and it rests on one trial with a commercial interest and no control during follow-up.

## Sleep, light and the body clock

**Days, not months.** One sleepless night lowered plasma glutathione the next morning [tbody-trivedi2017]. Six nights of 4 h in bed worsened glucose tolerance and raised evening cortisol, which normalised after 6 nights of long sleep [tbody-spiegel1999]. Ten nights of 4.2 h raised CRP [tbody-meierewert2004]. One week of 5-h nights lowered testosterone by roughly 10-15% [tbody-leproult2011]. Six weeks of 1.5 h less sleep increased endothelial oxidative stress [tbody-shah2023].

**Is one weekend enough?** Partly. After a week of 6-h nights, two 10-h nights brought IL-6 and sleepiness back to normal, but not attention [tbody-pejovic2013]. When the pattern repeats week after week, weekend catch-up did not prevent a 9-27% loss of insulin sensitivity [tbody-depner2019].

**Circadian disruption.** Three days of living 12 h out of phase raised blood pressure and IL-6, CRP and TNF-alpha [tbody-morris2016]. Three weeks of short, shifting sleep raised post-meal glucose, which normalised within 9 days of recovery [tbody-buxton2012]. Over decades, years of rotating night shifts raised coronary risk, which waned with years since quitting [tbody-vetter2016].

**Light.** Evening room light suppresses melatonin the same night [tbody-gooley2011], one night with the light on during sleep raised next-morning insulin resistance [tbody-mason2022], and five evenings of an e-reader delayed the clock [tbody-chang2015]. Morning and daytime light works just as fast in the other direction: a summer camping weekend achieved about 69% of a full week's shift [tbody-stothard2017], and a week aligned the clock to sunrise and sunset [tbody-wright2013]. Light therapy improves sleep problems over weeks [tbody-vanmaanen2016].

**Sleep apnoea and CPAP.** Stopping CPAP brought apnoea back within a few days and raised morning blood pressure by 8.5/6.9 mmHg within 2 weeks [tbody-kohler2011]. How quickly CPAP lowers oxidative stress is contested: 8-isoprostane fell after 12 weeks in one trial [tbody-alonsofernandez2009], but not after 2 months in a larger one [tbody-pazymar2016].

## Stress and meditation

During an exam period, seminal glutathione and sperm motility were lower than 3 months later [tbody-eskiocak2005], which fits the length of sperm production. Chronic stress over years is associated with higher oxidative stress and shorter telomeres [tbody-epel2004]. Most mind-body trials run 8-12 weeks and show modest falls in cortisol and blood pressure [tbody-pascoe2017]; one 3-day mindfulness retreat was followed by lower IL-6 four months later [tbody-creswell2016], but a systematic review calls the immune effects tentative [tbody-black2016].

## Glucose, metformin and rapamycin

**Glucose.** A 75 g glucose drink doubled white-cell ROS output at 2 h [tbody-mohanty2000]. When glucose is brought down, fasting glucose halves in about 6 days, fructosamine in 12 days, glycated albumin in 17 days and HbA1c in about 35 days, so HbA1c needs about 3 months to show the full change [tbody-tahara1995]. Collagen glycation accumulates over years [tbody-verzijl2000] and was 30% lower after about 5 years of intensive control [tbody-monnier1999].

**Metabolic memory.** In UKPDS, HbA1c differences between groups disappeared within a year after the trial, yet lower microvascular risk persisted and lower heart attack and death rates emerged over 10 years [tbody-holman2008], with no sign of waning at 24 years [tbody-adler2024]. In DCCT/EDIC, 6.5 years of intensive therapy cut cardiovascular events 42% over 17 years [tbody-nathan2005]. This is the strongest "lasting" effect in the group: early control pays off for decades.

**Metformin.** Its half-life is about 5 h [tbody-graham2011], so it is gone within a day or two of stopping. But taken during 12-14 weeks of training it blunted gains in insulin sensitivity, VO2max and muscle [tbody-konopka2019, tbody-walton2019], and its lowering of vitamin B12 grows over years (-19% at 4.3 years) [tbody-dejager2010, tbody-aroda2016]. In PCOS it helps ovulation, but less than letrozole or clomiphene [tbody-sharpe2019].

**Rapamycin.** The rationale for weekly dosing is that brief exposure inhibits mTORC1 (thought to carry the benefit) while sparing mTORC2 (which, when inhibited, causes insulin resistance); in mice, intermittent dosing spared glucose tolerance and immunity [tbody-lamming2012, tbody-arriolaapelo2016]. Sirolimus has a long half-life [tbody-mahalati2001]. In humans, a 6-week rapalog course improved vaccine response [tbody-mannick2014], and a 6-week TORC1-inhibitor combination was linked to fewer infections over the following year [tbody-mannick2018], but 48 weeks of 5-10 mg weekly rapamycin did not change visceral fat [tbody-moel2025]. No human trial has compared weekly and daily dosing.

## Sperm and egg: what the timing means here

Sperm take about 64 days (range 42-76) from labelling to the ejaculate [tbody-misell2006]; the egg's follicle grows for about 85 days from the pre-antral stage and is selected 15-20 days before ovulation [tbody-gougeon1986]. So almost any lifestyle change in this group needs **about 3 months** to act fully on a cycle's sperm or egg, and the reproductive time course is usually set by these biological clocks, not by the factor itself.

- **Exercise programmes and semen.** The only usable randomized trial (obese men, 16 weeks) improved count, motility and morphology [tbody-rosety2017]; a 14-week weight-loss programme was followed by higher sperm count [tbody-hakonsen2011]. The trials most often cited for exercise and semen come from an author group (Hajizadeh Maleki and Tartibian) with several retracted exercise-and-semen trials; they are not used here.
- **Too much training.** Very high running mileage is linked to lower testosterone and poorer semen [tbody-desouza1994]; in women, LH pulsatility is disrupted within 5 days of low energy availability [tbody-loucks2003], and when women ate about 330 kcal/day more, two-thirds of menstrual recoveries came within 3 months and 78% within 6 months [tbody-desouza2021]. Lean women doing 5+ h/week of vigorous exercise had lower fecundability [tbody-wise2012], but very active women did not end up more often childless, suggesting the harm reverses [tbody-gudmundsdottir2009].
- **Sleep.** Testosterone responds within a week [tbody-leproult2011], but sperm counts linked to sleep [tbody-coleman2026] would need a full cycle.
- **Glucose.** Birth-defect risk tracks HbA1c within 3 months around conception [tbody-ludvigsson2018], and HbA1c itself takes about 3 months to settle [tbody-tahara1995].

## Answers to the four questions

**1. Windows.** For sperm, a change made a month before ejaculation reaches only sperm in their final weeks of maturation, whereas a change made three months before covers the whole ~64-day production run [tbody-misell2006]; so for exercise, sleep, stress, cycling volume and glucose, starting at 3 months beats 2 weeks. For eggs, the follicle grows for about 85 days [tbody-gougeon1986], and energy-deficient women need months to restore cycles [tbody-desouza2021]. What can still change late: insulin sensitivity (days), testosterone with sleep (a week), circadian timing (days), post-meal glucose (per meal).

**2. Kinetics.** Fast (hours to days): post-meal glucose, ketone drinks, evening and morning light, single exercise bouts, late meals, one night of poor sleep, overfeeding. Build and plateau (weeks): mitochondria and antioxidant enzymes, insulin sensitivity with training, time-restricted eating, ketogenic adaptation, CPAP effects. Slow-build (months to years): VO2max, muscle, weight loss from any diet, fitness-related survival, collagen glycation.

**3. Persistence.** Most exercise effects are lost in weeks, but long-trained people keep capillaries and some enzymes [tbody-coyle1984] and much of their strength [tbody-psilander2019]. Weight lost on calorie restriction was half retained 2 years later [tbody-marlatt2017]. The biggest persistence is early glucose control, with a legacy effect for up to 24 years [tbody-adler2024], probably because glycated long-lived proteins turn over slowly [tbody-verzijl2000]. Harms persist too: shift-work heart risk fades only over years [tbody-vetter2016], while lab circadian disruption reverses within 9 days [tbody-buxton2012].

**4. Frequency.** Exercise needs to be at least every other day for the metabolic effect, because each bout's insulin effect lasts about 48 h [tbody-hawley2008], but fitness itself can be maintained with 2 sessions a week if intensity is kept [tbody-hickson1981] and strength with 1 [tbody-spiering2021]. Sleep, light, meal timing and post-meal walks act daily. Fasting schedules perform the same as daily restriction for the same deficit [tbody-allaf2021]. Rapamycin's weekly schedule is a mouse-based rationale [tbody-arriolaapelo2016].

## Where the evidence is contested

- **Does fitness cause the survival benefit?** Fitness change tracks mortality in cohorts [tbody-blair1995, tbody-lee2011], but Mendelian randomization found no clear causal effect of genetically higher VO2max on longevity [tbody-kjaergaard2025].
- **Frequent or concentrated?** The 48-h insulin effect argues for frequency [tbody-hawley2008], yet "weekend warriors" had similar mortality to people active most days [tbody-odonovan2017, tbody-dossantos2022].
- **Do antioxidants blunt training?** Blunting in several trials [tbody-ristow2009, tbody-gomezcabrera2008, tbody-paulsen2014] versus no effect in others and in a meta-analysis [tbody-yfanti2010, tbody-clifford2020]. Whether timing away from sessions helps is untested.
- **Muscle memory.** No faster regrowth in one study [tbody-psilander2019] versus persistent myonuclei and methylation favouring regrowth [tbody-cumming2024, tbody-seaborne2018].
- **Cold water and endurance.** Possible enhancement [tbody-ihsan2021] versus low-certainty evidence overall [tbody-berjisian2026].
- **Fasting beyond calories.** Weight-stable early time-restricted feeding helped in 5 weeks [tbody-sutton2018], but isocaloric TRE did not [tbody-peters2025] and calorie-matched TRE over a year added nothing significant [tbody-liu2022].
- **Weekend recovery sleep.** Reverses IL-6 after one week [tbody-pejovic2013] but not metabolic harm in a repeating pattern [tbody-depner2019].
- **CPAP and oxidative stress.** Fall in 8-isoprostane at 12 weeks [tbody-alonsofernandez2009] and higher antioxidant capacity [tbody-hosseini2023] versus no change at 2 months in a larger sham-controlled trial [tbody-pazymar2016] and no rise on withdrawal [tbody-stradling2015].
- **Glucose swings versus average.** Variability drove oxidative stress in type 2 diabetes and clamps [tbody-monnier2006, tbody-ceriello2008] but not in a type 1 study [tbody-wentholt2008].
- **Post-meal walk timing.** Walk straight after eating [tbody-reynolds2016] versus a meta-analytic hint that starting after the first hour lowers 24-h glucose more [tbody-kang2023].
- **Fasting-mimicking persistence.** Persisted 3 months, but follow-up was uncontrolled and the developer has a commercial interest [tbody-wei2017].
- **Rapamycin schedule.** Mouse rationale for intermittent dosing [tbody-arriolaapelo2016] versus a null year-long human trial [tbody-moel2025].
- **Stress and fertility.** Linked to poorer semen [tbody-nordkap2016] but not to IVF outcome in women [tbody-boivin2011].
- **Evening light in real life.** Lab effects within days [tbody-chang2015] versus doubts about real-life relevance [tbody-zeitzer2015].

## Practical bottom line

- **Start first (3+ months before trying to conceive, or now):** regular aerobic exercise and strength training, steady sleep of 7-9 h, glucose control, and enough food if training hard. These are the slow ones, and for sperm and eggs they need a full ~3-month cycle.
- **Needs to be daily or every other day:** exercise for its insulin effect, post-meal walks, sleep, dim evenings and bright mornings, regular meal times. These do not store up.
- **Can be done less often once built:** fitness (2 hard sessions a week keeps it), strength (1 session a week keeps it in younger adults).
- **Can wait or skip:** fasting schedules (no advantage over plain calorie control), ketone drinks (effects last hours), cold plunges (keep them away from lifting days), rapamycin (no proven human schedule).
- **Avoid during training blocks:** gram-dose vitamin C plus E; separating them from sessions is not proven to help.
- **Don't count on weekend catch-up:** it helps alertness and IL-6 after one bad week, not metabolism if short weeknights continue.

## What we don't know

- How quickly resting oxidative-damage markers (not just signalling) change with training; long trials are few.
- Whether antioxidant timing (far from sessions) avoids blunting.
- How long cold must be separated from lifting.
- How quickly fasting benefits revert after stopping, apart from weight.
- Human autophagy time courses during fasting.
- Direct semen time courses for cycling, sleep, and exercise programmes in non-obese men.
- Whether weekly rapamycin does anything useful in humans.
- How fast CPAP changes oxidative stress, given conflicting trials.
