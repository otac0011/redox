# Microplastics, PFAS & everyday chemical exposures

Plastics and "forever chemicals" are now found in nearly everyone. The hard questions are different from that headline: how much is really in our tissues, does it cause harm at the levels people carry, and can anything you do change your own levels? The answers differ a lot by chemical. For PFAS, the human evidence is fairly mature and there are tested ways to lower blood levels. For phthalates and bisphenols, simple swaps lower urine levels within days, but no trial shows a health benefit. For micro- and nanoplastics, even the measurements are disputed.

This page covers five exposures: micro- and nanoplastics, PFAS, phthalates and bisphenols ([plastics-chemicals]), personal lubricants and sperm, and pesticide residues in food. Sperm-specific pesticide data stay in [pesticides].

## The core science

All of these chemicals are linked to oxidative stress mainly in cell and animal studies. Plastic particles trigger mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), NADPH-oxidase activity and NLRP3/NF-kB inflammation in cultured cells and rodents [env-hernandez2025]. PFAS activate PPAR nuclear receptors, disturb lipid handling, and alter immune signalling, including through oxidative stress [env-ehrlich2023]. Bisphenols and phthalates are hormone disruptors that also raise ROS in experimental systems; in pregnant women, urinary phthalate metabolites tracked urinary 8-OHdG and 8-isoprostane across repeated samples [env-ferguson2015].

Two cautions apply throughout. First, short-lived chemicals (bisphenols, phthalates) clear from the body within hours, so a single urine sample is a noisy snapshot. Second, persistent chemicals (PFAS) are cleared partly through menstrual blood, pregnancy and breastfeeding, which can create misleading links with fertility (more on this below).

## 1. Micro- and nanoplastics

### What has been reported in human tissue

| Tissue | Method | Reported finding |
|---|---|---|
| Stool | FTIR spectroscopy | All 8 volunteers, median 20 particles per 10 g [env-schwabl2019] |
| Blood | Pyrolysis GC/MS | Mean 1.6 ug/mL in 22 donors, mainly PET, polyethylene, styrenes [env-leslie2022] |
| Placenta | Pyrolysis GC/MS | All 62 placentas, mean 127 ug/g, mostly polyethylene [env-garcia2024] |
| Testis | Pyrolysis GC/MS | All 23 human testes, mean 328 ug/g [env-hu2024] |
| Semen | Raman / infrared | A few particles per sample in most men [env-montano2023, env-li2024, env-zhao2023, env-guo2025] |
| Carotid plaque | Pyrolysis GC/MS | Polyethylene in 58% of plaques [env-marfella2024] |
| Brain | Pyrolysis GC/MS | Median ~4,900 ug/g in 2024 decedents, higher in dementia [env-nihart2025] |
| Olfactory bulb | Micro-FTIR | 16 particles in 8 of 15 decedents [env-amatolourenco2024] |

Look at the two columns together. Studies using pyrolysis GC/MS (which burns the sample and looks for chemical fragments of plastic) report large masses: the brain figure works out to about half a percent of tissue weight. Studies that look at and identify individual particles under a microscope find only a handful. Both cannot be right at face value.

### The measurement problem

Pyrolysis GC/MS identifies polyethylene by fragments that fats also produce. A validation study in human blood concluded that the method "is currently not a suitable analysis method" for polyethylene and PVC in biological samples, because tissue components create false positives and realistic detection limits are up to 20 times higher than advertised [env-rauert2025]. Choosing longer-chain markers that fats cannot mimic raises the polyethylene detection limit to about 50 ug/g [env-almeida2026], which is in the range many human studies report. A quality-control screen of 133 datasets from 101 human-tissue studies found that none met all essential criteria; missing blank documentation, missing positive controls and unrepresentative sampling were common [env-lane2026]. Analytical chemists have proposed requiring two or more independent techniques before claiming plastic identification [env-thomas2026].

The specific landmark papers have been challenged:

- **Testis (Hu 2024).** A published comment listed missing blanks, controls, unvalidated digestion steps and no detection limits [env-uppu2025]. The authors replied that controls were described in an earlier paper, but also said that only 6 of their 12 polymers give enough confidence for health work [env-hu2025].
- **Brain (Nihart 2025).** The paper was corrected for duplicated supplementary images, wrong scale bars and an omitted preparation step [env-nihart2025b], and microplastic analysts published correspondence on the challenges of measuring plastic in brain [env-amonikh2025]. The authors themselves note that dementia brains may hold more plastic because of poorer clearance, not the reverse [env-nihart2025].
- **Teabags (Hernandez 2019).** The claim that one plastic teabag releases about 15 billion particles [env-hernandez2019] drew a published comment and response [env-busse2020].

### Heart attacks and strokes

The strongest human outcome study followed 257 people who had carotid plaque removed. Those whose plaque contained detectable polyethylene had 4.5 times the rate of heart attack, stroke or death over about 34 months (HR 4.53, 95% CI 2.00-10.27) [env-marfella2024]. This is a striking result, but it is one observational cohort, it measured exactly the two polymers (polyethylene and PVC) that are most vulnerable to false positives [env-rauert2025], and it cannot separate plastic from other exposures that travel with it. Several letters were published with an author reply [env-glazier2024, env-wang2024, env-kalkman2024, env-holda2024, env-marfella2024b]. Replication in an independent cohort with contamination-controlled methods is the key missing step.

### Sperm and eggs

Plastic in testis and semen is reported [env-hu2024, env-zhao2023], but evidence of harm in men is thin: a borderline lower motility in 45 fertility patients with PET particles (20.6% vs 34.9%, p = 0.056) [env-guo2025] and motility differences between polymer types in 40 men [env-li2024]. No human study has measured sperm DNA fragmentation against microplastic exposure. Animal studies frequently report testicular and ovarian harm, but a systematic quality appraisal of 102 studies found that particle characterization, dose and dose-response were often missing [env-due2026]. See [microplastics-sperm].

### Where exposure comes from

| Source | What was measured |
|---|---|
| Bottled water | About 240,000 particles per litre, 90% nanoplastics, by single-particle Raman imaging [env-qian2024]; bottled-only drinkers estimated to take in ~90,000 extra particles a year vs ~4,000 for tap [env-cox2019] |
| Microwaving in plastic | Up to 4.2 million micro- and 2.1 billion nanoplastic particles per cm2 of container in 3 minutes [env-hussain2023] |
| Infant bottles | Polypropylene bottles released up to 16 million particles per litre during formula preparation, more with hot water and sterilizing [env-lid2020] |
| Plastic teabags | ~11.6 billion micro- and 3.1 billion nanoparticles per cup at 95 C [env-hernandez2019] |
| House dust | Synthetic textile fibres common; more vacuuming, less dust plastic [env-soltani2022] |
| Food overall | 39,000-52,000 particles a year from food in US estimates, more with inhalation [env-cox2019] |

These are release or content measurements. Whether swallowed particles are absorbed, and in what amounts, is unknown.

### Can you lower your body burden?

Unknown. No published human study has shown that any change (glass containers, filtered water, avoiding bottled water) lowers microplastic levels in blood or tissue. An umbrella review of meta-analyses on plastic-associated chemicals found no meta-analyses at all on microplastics and human health [env-symeonides2024]. A randomized trial of a 4-week low-plastic diet and lifestyle change is under way, but it measures urinary bisphenols and phthalates, not particles [env-lucas2025]. The steps listed in [microplastics] are reasonable because they cost little and also cut [plastics-chemicals], not because they are proven.

## 2. PFAS

PFAS are fluorinated chemicals used in non-stick coatings, stain- and water-repellent fabrics, food packaging and firefighting foam. The long-chain ones persist: after contaminated water was replaced in Ronneby, Sweden, serum PFHxS, PFOS and PFOA fell only 13%, 20% and 26% a year (half-lives 5.3, 3.4 and 2.7 years), faster in women [env-li2018].

### Human evidence and clinical guidance

The US National Academies (NASEM) concluded in 2022 that PFAS exposure is linked to certain cancers, thyroid dysfunction, cholesterol changes and small reductions in birth weight, and recommended blood testing for people likely to have high exposure [env-nasem2022]:

| Sum of 7 PFAS in serum (MeFOSAA, PFHxS, PFOA, PFDA, PFUnDA, PFOS, PFNA) | NASEM interpretation | Suggested clinical action |
|---|---|---|
| Below 2 ng/mL | Adverse effects not expected | Usual care |
| 2-20 ng/mL | Potential for adverse effects, especially in sensitive groups | Reduce exposure; lipid screening; blood-pressure monitoring in pregnancy; usual breast cancer screening |
| Above 20 ng/mL | Increased risk | Reduce exposure; lipid screening, thyroid testing, kidney and testicular cancer and ulcerative colitis assessment |

The European Food Safety Authority set a tolerable weekly intake of 4.4 ng/kg body weight for the sum of PFOA, PFNA, PFHxS and PFOS, based on immune effects, and said part of the European population exceeds it [env-efsa2020]. The immune signal comes largely from Faroese children, in whom a doubling of serum PFAS was associated with 49% lower tetanus and diphtheria antibody levels [env-grandjean2012]; experimental work supports immune effects through several pathways [env-ehrlich2023].

On cholesterol, the picture is less settled than the guidance suggests. A 2025 meta-analysis that focused on bias found pooled links between PFOA/PFOS and higher total and LDL cholesterol, but these came mostly from cross-sectional studies; longitudinal estimates trended toward null [env-hussey2025].

### Oxidative stress

In 469 pregnant women, each interquartile rise in PFOS went with 9.7% higher urinary 8-isoprostane, though another PFAS showed the opposite direction [env-siwakoti2024]. In 597 Taiwanese adults, linear PFOS tracked higher urinary 8-OHdG [env-lin2020]. But a meta-analysis of 49 PFAS studies found PFAS-oxidative stress associations to be mostly inconsistent; organochlorine pesticides, not PFAS, showed the clearest signal [env-chen2023]. PFAS are not mainly an "oxidative stress" exposure in humans as far as current data show.

### Fertility

**Sperm.** A 2026 meta-analysis of 14 studies (7,465 men) found PFAS weakly linked to lower progressive motility (r = -0.058) and more DNA fragmentation (r = 0.141) [env-dinisio2026]; an earlier one found PFOA and PFNA tied to lower motility [env-wangh2023a]. Young Danish men with high PFOS+PFOA had fewer normal sperm [env-joensen2009]. But a 604-man study found no consistent link with sperm DNA fragmentation [env-specht2012], the US LIFE study found associations pointing in different directions [env-louis2015], and a systematic review found little consistent evidence in men [env-bach2016]. See [pfas-sperm].

**Eggs and conception.** Early birth-cohort studies linked higher PFOS/PFOA to longer time to pregnancy [env-fei2009]. Then a Norwegian study found the link only in women who had been pregnant before, not in first-time mothers (OR 0.5-0.7) [env-whitworth2012]. The explanation is reverse causation: pregnancy and breastfeeding lower a woman's PFAS, so a long gap since the last pregnancy (as happens with slow conception) lets PFAS build up [env-fei2012]. A systematic review judged the association most likely not causal [env-bach2016]. Newer work in women still trying to conceive, which avoids this bias, found 5-10% lower fecundability per quartile of PFDA and PFOS [env-cohen2023]. In Chinese clinic studies, a PFAS mixture led by newer replacement chemicals was associated with PCOS [env-zhan2023], though an earlier study found only one congener linked [env-wangw2019]. Follicular-fluid PFOA was associated with poorer embryo quality in IVF [env-xu2025], while another IVF study found no clear outcome pattern despite PFAS passing easily into follicular fluid [env-hong2022]. In ovarian tissue, PFAS were not linked to AMH or follicle density [env-bjorvang2021]. See [pfas-egg].

### Drinking water and filtration

Drinking water is a major route where it is contaminated. In Southern California, people whose public water had detectable PFAS had higher serum levels (PFHxS about 80% higher with detections in treated water) [env-fillman2026]. An evidence summary found clear water-to-blood links at 10-100 ng/L, especially for PFHxS and in children and heavy water drinkers, but inconsistent links below about 10 ng/L [env-tefera2026]. In a North Carolina county with decades of contaminated river water, residents who filtered their tap water at home had lower serum levels of all seven PFAS measured [env-cuffney2023].

Household devices use activated carbon, ion exchange or reverse osmosis. A 2024 review found they can remove PFAS and many are certified for PFOA/PFOS, but only five studies tested them in real homes, short-chain PFAS are harder to remove, and poorly maintained carbon filters can even raise PFAS in the water [env-mackeown2024]. See [water-filtration].

### Getting PFAS out: blood and plasma donation, and drugs

| Intervention | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Plasma donation every 6 weeks for 12 months | RCT, 285 firefighters, PFOS >=5 ng/mL | PFOS -2.9 ng/mL, PFHxS -1.1 ng/mL; no change with observation [env-gasiorowski2022] |
| Whole-blood donation every 12 weeks for 12 months | Same RCT | PFOS -1.1 ng/mL; PFHxS unchanged [env-gasiorowski2022] |
| Cholestyramine 4 g three times daily, 12 weeks | Randomized crossover, 45 highly exposed adults | PFOS -60% from a median of 191 ng/mL; other PFAS -15 to -44% [env-moller2024] |
| Colesevelam, 12 weeks | Crossover, 10 adults | PFOS -38% vs -2% in control period [env-andersson2025] |
| Plasma donation and/or cholestyramine | Uncontrolled program, 19 treated firefighters | Apparent PFOS half-life 1.2 vs 7.3 years untreated [env-delaere2025] |
| Dietary fibre, 4 weeks | Secondary analysis of a cholesterol RCT, 72 men | Long-chain PFAS fell only in the fibre group [env-schlezinger2025] |

Removing blood removes the PFAS bound to its proteins, and bile-acid binders interrupt PFAS recycling between bile and gut. None of these studies measured health outcomes, so the benefit of lowering PFAS this way is assumed rather than shown. Donation should be done only by eligible donors and for its own sake; cholestyramine is a prescription drug with digestive side effects that also blocks absorption of other medicines.

## 3. Phthalates and bisphenols (update to [plastics-chemicals])

### Do exposure-reduction studies work?

| Study | Intervention | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Rudel 2011, 20 people | 3 days of fresh, unpackaged food | Urinary BPA -66%, DEHP metabolites -53 to -56% [env-rudel2011] |
| Sathyanarayana 2013, RCT, 40 people | 5 days of catered "low-exposure" food | DEHP metabolites rose about 25-fold; BPA rose too; contaminated coriander and milk [env-sathyanarayana2013] |
| Carwile 2011, crossover RCT, 75 adults | Canned vs fresh soup daily for 5 days | Urinary BPA 20.8 vs 1.1 ug/L [env-carwile2011] |
| Carwile 2009, 77 students | 1 week of polycarbonate bottles | Urinary BPA +69% [env-carwile2009] |
| Peng 2019, crossover RCT, 20 adults | One canned vs fresh meal | BPA up to +206% at 4 h, back to baseline by 24 h [env-peng2019] |
| Plachetka 2025, crossover RCT, 103 women | Soda from cans vs PET vs glass | BPA +22% after cans vs PET; BPS, BPF found regardless of packaging [env-plachetka2025] |
| HERMOSA 2016, 100 girls | 3 days of labelled-free personal-care products | MEP -27%, methyl/propyl paraben ~-44%, triclosan and BP-3 ~-36% [env-harley2016] |

The pattern is clear: removing a known source quickly lowers urine levels, and adding one quickly raises them. The Sathyanarayana trial is the important counterweight. Even a carefully designed diet failed because ordinary ingredients were contaminated, which suggests that individual effort has limits when the food supply itself carries these chemicals [env-sathyanarayana2013]. No exposure-reduction study has yet measured a health or fertility outcome.

### Safe dose, and BPA substitutes

In 2023 the European Food Safety Authority cut its tolerable daily intake for BPA 20,000-fold, from 4 ug/kg to 0.2 ng/kg body weight per day, based on immune (Th17 cell) effects in mice, and concluded that average dietary exposure exceeds it by two to three orders of magnitude [env-efsa2023]. Other regulators (Germany's BfR, the US FDA) did not follow; academic endocrine scientists argue those agencies rely too heavily on industry-run guideline studies [env-vomsaal2024].

"BPA-free" products usually swap in BPS or BPF. Reviews find reproductive-toxicity and hormone-disruption signals for BPS, BPAF and to a lesser extent BPF, and very little data for most other alternatives [env-denbraversewradj2020]; BPS and BPF are now themselves being regulated, and newer substitutes carry large data gaps [env-mhaoutykodja2024].

### Fertility

An umbrella review of 52 systematic reviews linked phthalates to pregnancy loss and reduced sperm quality, and BPA to PCOS, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease, though most reviews were of moderate quality [env-symeonides2024]. Individual meta-analyses are less tidy. For BPA and semen, one found a weak motility link that disappeared after correcting for publication bias [env-castellini2022]; another found small falls in concentration and count [env-lu2024]. For phthalates, only MBP, MBzP and semen DEHP tracked lower sperm concentration [env-wangh2023b]. In IVF, BPA was linked to lower fertilization rates and fewer good embryos but not to pregnancy rates [env-yuan2025]. Time-to-pregnancy cohorts conflict: one found bisphenols and phthalates prolonged it [env-charifson2024]; another found BPS prolonged it but several phthalates shortened it, and called its own findings hypothesis-generating [env-blaauwendraad2025].

## 4. Personal lubricants and sperm

| Product | In vitro finding |
|---|---|
| K-Y Jelly | Reduced sperm head movement [env-anderson1998]; raised DNA fragmentation [env-agarwal2008] |
| Astroglide, FemGlide, Replens | Reduced progressive motility; FemGlide also raised DNA fragmentation [env-agarwal2008] |
| Olive oil, saliva | Reduced motility even at 6.25% concentration [env-anderson1998]; saliva harmful even at low concentration [env-tulandi1982] and worse than pleasure gels [env-tomlinson2023] |
| Baby oil | No significant effect [env-anderson1998] |
| Aquagel (clinic gel) | Motility down to 49% of control within 10 minutes [env-mackenzie2019] |
| Pre-Seed and other "sperm-friendly" gels | No effect on motility or DNA fragmentation [env-agarwal2008]; best motility and vitality of nine products [env-mowat2014] |

Parabens at concentrations used in some lubricants raised mitochondrial ROS and oxidative DNA damage in sperm in the lab [env-samarasinghe2018]. When Pre-Seed was used during masturbation for semen collection, it did not change motility, ROS or DNA damage [env-agarwal2013].

Now the real-world data. In 296 women trying to conceive, using lubricant in the fertile window did not lower fecundability (ratio 1.05, 95% CI 0.59-1.85) [env-steiner2012]. In 6,467 pregnancy planners in Denmark and North America, no lubricant type was linked to lower fecundability: water-based 1.02, "fertility-friendly" 1.01, oil-based 1.23, silicone 1.27 [env-mcinerney2018].

**How to read the gap.** Lab tests mix washed sperm directly with 5-12.5% lubricant and watch for up to a few hours. During intercourse, most sperm that will fertilize enter cervical mucus within minutes and may never meet much lubricant. The lab data are real but measure a different situation. The cohort data are the better guide for natural conception, but they rely on self-report and no randomized trial exists [env-mesen2014]. A 2024 systematic review found only 2 of 24 studies were in vivo and concluded there is not enough evidence for a "sperm-safe" guideline [env-gumerova2024]. Where the lab data clearly matter is semen collected for analysis, IUI or IVF, where lubricant is in direct contact with the whole sample. See [lubricants-sperm].

## 5. Pesticides in food and organic diets

Organic food reliably lowers pesticide exposure. Switching four US families to organic food cut urinary levels of 13 pesticides or metabolites, including a malathion metabolite by 95% and a chlorpyrifos metabolite by 61% [env-hyland2019]. A randomized organic-diet study in adults lowered total organophosphate metabolites by about 75% [env-gonzalez2023]. In pregnant women, an organic week lowered glyphosate modestly, and only in those living away from farm fields [env-hyland2023].

Health outcomes are much less certain. In 68,946 French adults, the highest organic-food consumers had 25% lower cancer risk, an absolute difference of 0.6% [env-baudry2018]; an accompanying commentary questioned whether this justifies the cost [env-hemler2018], and a later analysis in the same cohort found substitution of organic produce linked to lower postmenopausal breast cancer only [env-berlivet2026]. Systematic reviews disagree: one reports lower cardiometabolic risk in prospective studies [env-poulia2025], the other finds results contradictory and inconclusive [env-komati2025]. Organic buyers differ from others in income, weight, exercise and overall diet, which cohort adjustment cannot fully remove.

For fertility, women eating the most high-residue produce had 26% lower probability of live birth after IVF [env-chiu2018], and male partners' high-residue produce intake tracked lower fertilization in conventional IVF [env-kazemi2025]. But in 5,234 women trying to conceive naturally, residue intake was unrelated to fecundability, and the only inverse links appeared in a subgroup where reverse causation was likely [env-wesselink2020]. Semen findings are in [pesticides]. See [organic-food]. The net effect of eating fruit and vegetables is positive ([high-fruit-vegetable-intake]); eating less produce to avoid residues is a bad trade.

## Sperm and egg relevance at a glance

| Exposure | Sperm | Egg / female fertility |
|---|---|---|
| Microplastics | Present in testis and semen; harm unproven in humans | Too little human data |
| PFAS | Small links with motility and DNA fragmentation; inconsistent | Modestly lower fecundability in a preconception cohort; PCOS and embryo-quality links in clinic studies; reverse causation in older studies |
| Phthalates/BPA | Weak, partly publication-bias-driven links | BPA linked to lower IVF fertilization and embryo quality |
| Lubricants | Many impair sperm in vitro | Not linked to lower fecundability in cohorts |
| Pesticide residues | See [pesticides] | High-residue produce linked to lower IVF live birth, not natural fecundability |

## Where the evidence is contested

1. **How much plastic is really in human tissue.** Py-GC/MS papers report hundreds to thousands of ug/g [env-nihart2025, env-hu2024, env-garcia2024]. Methods researchers say the technique overcounts polyethylene and PVC in fatty tissue [env-rauert2025, env-almeida2026], no human study meets all quality criteria [env-lane2026], and particle-counting methods find far less [env-amatolourenco2024]. The testis and brain papers drew a formal comment and correspondence respectively [env-uppu2025, env-amonikh2025], and the brain paper was corrected [env-nihart2025b]. The testis authors conceded that only half their polymers are reliable [env-hu2025].
2. **Plastic in plaque and cardiovascular events.** A single NEJM cohort found HR 4.53 [env-marfella2024], with letters and a reply published [env-glazier2024, env-wang2024, env-kalkman2024, env-holda2024, env-marfella2024b]. No independent replication exists, and the polymers measured are the ones most prone to false positives [env-rauert2025].
3. **Animal toxicity of plastic particles.** Many studies report reproductive harm, but most are of limited regulatory reliability and use pristine particles at high doses [env-due2026, env-hernandez2025].
4. **PFAS and fertility in women.** Longer time to pregnancy [env-fei2009] looks like reverse causation from parity [env-whitworth2012, env-fei2012, env-bach2016], yet a preconception cohort still finds a modest effect [env-cohen2023]. PCOS and IVF findings conflict [env-zhan2023, env-wangw2019, env-xu2025, env-hong2022].
5. **PFAS and sperm.** Pooled motility and DNA-fragmentation links [env-dinisio2026, env-wangh2023a] versus null or mixed large studies [env-specht2012, env-louis2015, env-bach2016].
6. **PFAS and cholesterol.** Guidance treats the link as established [env-nasem2022]; a bias-focused meta-analysis finds longitudinal data near null [env-hussey2025].
7. **PFAS and oxidative stress.** Positive single cohorts [env-siwakoti2024, env-lin2020] versus a meta-analysis finding mostly inconsistent results [env-chen2023].
8. **The BPA safe dose.** EFSA's 20,000-fold cut [env-efsa2023] is endorsed by academic endocrinologists [env-vomsaal2024] and not adopted by other regulators.
9. **Whether personal action works for phthalates and BPA.** Fresh-food swaps worked [env-rudel2011]; a randomized diet trial backfired [env-sathyanarayana2013].
10. **BPA, phthalates and semen.** Publication-bias-adjusted null [env-castellini2022] versus small count reductions [env-lu2024]; time-to-pregnancy cohorts point in opposite directions [env-charifson2024, env-blaauwendraad2025].
11. **Lubricants.** Lab harm [env-anderson1998, env-agarwal2008] versus no real-world effect on conception [env-steiner2012, env-mcinerney2018].
12. **Organic food.** Clear exposure reduction [env-hyland2019] but confounded health outcomes [env-baudry2018, env-hemler2018, env-komati2025]; IVF links [env-chiu2018] not seen in natural conception [env-wesselink2020].

## Practical bottom line

- **PFAS (best evidence for action).** If you have likely high exposure (contaminated local water, firefighting, PFAS industry), ask about a serum PFAS test and use the NASEM 2 and 20 ng/mL cut-offs [env-nasem2022]. If your water report shows PFAS, especially above about 10 ng/L, use a reverse-osmosis or certified carbon filter and change cartridges on time [env-tefera2026, env-mackeown2024]. Eligible donors who already give plasma or blood lower their PFOS as a side effect [env-gasiorowski2022]. Cholestyramine is an option to discuss with a doctor only for very high levels [env-moller2024].
- **Phthalates and bisphenols.** Eat less canned and plastic-packaged food, don't drink from polycarbonate, and pick fragrance-free or phthalate/paraben-free personal-care products. These steps lower urine levels within days [env-rudel2011, env-carwile2011, env-harley2016]. Don't expect measurable health changes; none has been shown.
- **Microplastics.** Don't heat food, drinks or infant formula in plastic; use glass or steel for hot food; prefer safe tap water over bottled; avoid plastic teabags [env-hussain2023, env-lid2020, env-qian2024, env-hernandez2019]. These are low-cost precautions, not proven risk reducers.
- **Lubricants.** For natural conception, lubricant use does not appear to lower your chances [env-mcinerney2018]. If you want extra caution, or for any semen sample for testing, IUI or IVF, use an isotonic "fertility-friendly" product and avoid saliva and oils [env-agarwal2013, env-anderson1998].
- **Pesticides.** Keep eating plenty of fruit and vegetables. Choose organic for high-residue items if affordable, mainly for exposure reduction [env-hyland2019].

## What we don't know

- Whether micro- and nanoplastics are absorbed in meaningful amounts, how much is really in organs, how long it stays, and whether it causes any human disease.
- Whether any personal change lowers microplastic body burden; no study has tested this.
- Whether lowering serum PFAS by donation or drugs improves any health outcome, including fertility.
- Whether household filters lower serum PFAS at the low water concentrations most people have.
- Whether reducing phthalate or bisphenol exposure improves semen quality, egg quality or time to pregnancy; the first randomized low-plastic trial measures urinary chemicals and cardiometabolic markers [env-lucas2025].
- How safe the newer PFAS and BPA replacements are.
- Whether lubricants matter for couples with marginal sperm quality; no randomized trial exists.
- Whether organic diets improve long-term health beyond exposure markers.
