# Diet claims under the microscope: confounding, newer trials and what still holds (audit, October 2026)

This is an audit of the 57 food, diet-pattern and fasting entries on Redox Compass. For each one we asked two questions. First, does the headline rest on observational data that newer, better-designed studies have tested, and did it survive? Second, is there newer evidence (meta-analyses, large trials, guideline changes) that the page does not yet reflect? Fifty-one entries were audited; six were skipped because they make only food-chemistry or pharmacokinetic claims with nothing contested to test (eggplant, cooking-oil choice, herbs and spices, marinating meat, mustard-seed myrosinase, wasabi/horseradish).

The short version: most of the site's diet entries hold up, largely because they were already written cautiously. Seven claims should be softened, three are better supported now than the page says, and none is overturned.

## How confounding works in nutrition research

Nutrition is the classic home of confounding because what people eat travels with almost everything else about them. Someone who drinks three cups of filtered coffee, eats whole-grain bread and five servings of vegetables is more likely to be educated, non-smoking, insured and active. Cohort studies adjust for the things they measured, but never perfectly (residual confounding). Diet is also measured badly: food-frequency questionnaires ask people to recall a year of eating, and the errors are large and not random.

Four patterns recur.

1. **Healthy-user and sick-quitter effects.** People who stop drinking coffee or alcohol often stop because they are ill. If they are counted as "non-drinkers", moderate drinkers look protected. When a 2023 meta-analysis of 107 alcohol cohorts separated former drinkers from lifetime abstainers, the famous protective dip in mortality for light drinking disappeared [adiet-zhao2023]. A UK Biobank study found light drinkers simply had healthier lifestyles, and genetic analysis showed alcohol raised blood pressure and coronary risk at every level [adiet-biddinger2022].
2. **Randomized trials that overturned cohort findings.** Carotenoid-rich diets looked protective, but beta-carotene pills increased lung cancer and death in smokers in the CARET trial (1996) [adiet-omenn1996], and Cochrane later found beta-carotene and vitamin E supplements raised mortality [adiet-bjelakovic2012]. More recently, the MIND diet was linked to 53% less Alzheimer disease in cohorts, yet the 3-year MIND trial (2023) found no cognitive benefit [adiet-barnes2023]. A small 10-patient pomegranate study was not borne out by an 18-month trial of 289 people [adiet-davidson2009].
3. **Mendelian randomization (MR).** MR uses genes that nudge an exposure up or down as a natural experiment. Genes that make people drink more coffee were not linked to less heart disease or death [adiet-nordestgaard2016, adiet-nordestgaard2022]. But MR has its own traps in nutrition. Genetic "instruments" for food choices are weak and often reflect taste, smell and education rather than the food itself; a UK Biobank analysis found the "healthy" wholemeal-bread pattern was driven by education-linked genetics [adiet-cole2020]. Fatty-acid MR depends heavily on one gene cluster (FADS) that also changes cholesterol, so results can be biased by pleiotropy [adiet-borges2022, adiet-yuan2019].
4. **Natural experiments.** When New York counties restricted trans fat, heart attack and stroke admissions fell 6.2% more than in similar counties without restrictions [adiet-brandt2017], which supports causation without relying on individuals' self-reported diets.

The field itself is split on how much to trust cohorts. Ioannidis argues that tiny effects, massive confounding and poor measurement make most observational nutrition findings unreliable [adiet-ioannidis2018, adiet-trepanowski2018]; a review of 50 random cookbook ingredients found 80% had been linked to cancer, with single-study effects that shrank to near-null in meta-analyses [adiet-schoenfeld2013]. Willett, Hu and colleagues reply that drug-trial standards do not fit lifelong dietary exposures, and that repeated measures, biomarkers and consistency across designs can support causal conclusions [adiet-satija2015, adiet-qian2020]. Both critiques shape the verdicts below.

## The most important reversals and confirmations

**Coffee (MR 2016, review 2022).** Cohorts consistently find about 15% lower mortality at 3-4 cups a day, and the association survives stratification by smoking [adiet-kim2019, adiet-poole2017]. But genetic studies do not support a causal benefit for mortality, heart disease, stroke or diabetes [adiet-nordestgaard2016, adiet-nordestgaard2022]. UK Biobank shows the same benefit with decaf and instant coffee and higher risk at more than 6 cups a day [adiet-liu2022, adiet-zhou2019]. A randomized n-of-1 trial (2023) found coffee did not raise atrial ectopy but did increase ventricular ectopy and shorten sleep by about 36 minutes [adiet-marcus2023]. Coffee is safe; the life-extension claim is uncertain.

**Red and processed meat (2019-2025).** The NutriRECS panel (2019) rated the evidence low certainty and told adults to continue current intake [adiet-johnston2019, adiet-zeraatkar2019], drawing a sharp rebuttal [adiet-qian2020]. Two independent Burden of Proof analyses since then, which build between-study disagreement into their estimates, found only weak evidence for unprocessed red meat [adiet-lescinsky2022] and a modest but consistent link for processed meat (at least 11% higher diabetes and 7% higher colorectal cancer risk) [adiet-haile2025]. Substitution matters: replacing red meat with nuts, legumes or soy is associated with lower heart disease [adiet-alshaar2020]. IARC's Group 1 label [adiet-bouvard2015] measures how sure we are that a hazard exists, not how big it is.

**PREDIMED and the Mediterranean diet (2018-2022).** The landmark 2013 trial was retracted and republished in 2018 after it emerged that some participants had not been randomized [adiet-estruch2018r]. The re-analysis held [adiet-estruch2018], but Agarwal and Ioannidis questioned whether it can still be trusted as randomized evidence [adiet-agarwal2019], and Cochrane rated the mortality evidence low quality while finding fewer strokes [adiet-rees2019]. Cochrane also set aside two trials over data-reliability concerns; separately, the Indo-Mediterranean Diet Heart Study carries a Lancet expression of concern [adiet-singh2002]. The 2022 CORDIOPREV trial, 1,002 heart patients over 7 years, is the most important new confirmation: about a quarter fewer cardiovascular events than with a low-fat diet [adiet-delgadolista2022], alongside the older Lyon trial [adiet-delorgeril1999]. For olive oil specifically, the evidence is weaker than it looks: PREDIMED tested the whole diet, and in US cohorts olive oil beat butter and margarine but not other vegetable oils [adiet-guaschferre2022].

**Seed oils (2010-2022).** The site already presents both sides well [adiet-ramsden2013, adiet-ramsden2016, adiet-li2020, adiet-marklund2019]. Two items are missing. Ramsden's 2010 trial meta-analysis showed that trials raising omega-6 alone gave no benefit (RR 1.13), while trials that raised omega-3 too did [adiet-ramsden2010]. And the largest MR study (2022) did not find a protective effect of genetically higher linoleic acid on heart disease, though FADS pleiotropy limits it [adiet-borges2022]. On the other side, pooled biomarker cohorts link higher linoleic acid with 35% less type 2 diabetes [adiet-wu2017]. Net: no sign of toxicity, and replacing saturated fat is supported [adiet-hooper2020], but extra linoleic acid on top is not proven to help [adiet-hooper2018].

**Alcohol and red wine (2022-2023).** The protective J-curve has largely dissolved under bias correction and MR [adiet-zhao2023, adiet-biddinger2022]. The site's "harmful" rating for red wine is now better supported than when it was written. Resveratrol barely reaches the blood [adiet-walle2004], urinary levels did not predict survival [adiet-semba2014], and alcohol itself raises lipid peroxidation [adiet-meagher1999].

**Trans fat (2017, 2025).** The New York natural experiment [adiet-brandt2017] adds a design that does not depend on questionnaires, though the 2025 Burden of Proof review still gave the dose-response only two stars [adiet-haile2025].

## Where the evidence is contested

- **Whether nutrition cohorts can be trusted at all.** Ioannidis and colleagues argue for replacing most observational nutrition research with large trials [adiet-ioannidis2018, adiet-trepanowski2018, adiet-schoenfeld2013]; Satija, Willett, Hu and colleagues argue that well-run cohorts with repeated measures are the only practical tool for lifelong diet and that GRADE penalizes them unfairly [adiet-satija2015, adiet-qian2020].
- **Red meat.** NutriRECS [adiet-johnston2019] versus Harvard nutrition epidemiologists [adiet-qian2020]; the Burden of Proof results [adiet-lescinsky2022, adiet-haile2025] sit between them.
- **Coffee.** Robust, smoking-stratified cohort associations [adiet-kim2019] versus null MR [adiet-nordestgaard2016]. MR for coffee is itself contested because caffeine-metabolism genes change both intake and blood caffeine.
- **Linoleic acid.** Cohort and biomarker benefit [adiet-li2020, adiet-marklund2019, adiet-wu2017] and the AHA advisory [adiet-sacks2017] versus recovered trials, n-6-only trial meta-analysis and MR [adiet-ramsden2013, adiet-ramsden2016, adiet-ramsden2010, adiet-borges2022]. UK Biobank finds both plasma omega-6 and omega-3 associated with lower mortality, while a high omega-6/omega-3 ratio looks worse [adiet-zhang2024].
- **PREDIMED's trustworthiness.** The investigators' republication [adiet-estruch2018] versus Agarwal and Ioannidis [adiet-agarwal2019]; CORDIOPREV [adiet-delgadolista2022] partly settles the bigger question of whether the Mediterranean pattern works.
- **Whole grains.** Strong cohort associations [adiet-aune2016] versus no outcome trials and no clear lipid effect in Cochrane's trial review [adiet-kelly2017], plus evidence that the bread-choice signal tracks education [adiet-cole2020].
- **Sulforaphane.** A striking 2014 autism trial [adiet-singh2014] was followed by three trials that missed their primary or caregiver-rated outcomes [adiet-zimmerman2021, adiet-magner2023, adiet-ou2024]; concentrated sulforaphane did nothing to Nrf2 or oxidative markers in haemodialysis [adiet-ribeiro2024] or COPD [adiet-wise2016]; and a prediabetes trial missed its primary endpoint, with benefit only in exploratory responders [adiet-dwibedi2025]. The detoxication trials in Qidong remain the strongest positive evidence [adiet-egner2014]. The broader question of whether Nrf2 activators deliver in humans is reviewed in [adiet-houghton2016].
- **Omega-3.** Fish eaters do better in cohorts [adiet-mozaffarian2006], but supplement trials are mostly null for heart events [adiet-manson2019, adiet-abdelhamid2020] and raise atrial fibrillation [adiet-gencer2021, adiet-chen2024]. Fish also carries methylmercury, which may blunt its benefit [adiet-mozaffarian2006]. Small RCTs support omega-3 for sperm parameters [adiet-salashuetos2018, adiet-martinezsoto2016].
- **Sugary drinks and semen.** A 2024 preconception cohort found about 22% lower sperm concentration with seven or more drinks a week [adiet-joseph2024], and sugary drinks were linked to lower fecundability [adiet-hatch2018], but a Danish study of 1,047 young men found no strong association [adiet-meldgaard2022].
- **Mediterranean diet and fertility.** Two small RCTs found better sperm counts [adiet-caruso2020, adiet-ghasemi2025] and observational pooling agrees [adiet-agarwalr2025], but for IVF outcomes the evidence is a single strong cohort [adiet-karayiannis2018] and a meta-analysis that judged it insufficient [adiet-muffone2023].

## What changed recently (2021-2026)

- 2020-2021: Mediterranean-diet sperm RCT [adiet-caruso2020]; fruit-and-vegetable mortality plateau at about 5 servings/day [adiet-wang2021]; omega-3 atrial fibrillation meta-analysis [adiet-gencer2021].
- 2022: CORDIOPREV [adiet-delgadolista2022]; Burden of Proof on unprocessed red meat [adiet-lescinsky2022]; coffee MR review [adiet-nordestgaard2022]; alcohol MR [adiet-biddinger2022]; calorie-matched TRE trial [adiet-liu2022c].
- 2023: MIND trial null [adiet-barnes2023]; alcohol abstainer-bias meta-analysis [adiet-zhao2023]; CRAVE coffee trial [adiet-marcus2023]; flaxseed and nitrate meta-analyses showing smaller BP effects [adiet-li2023, adiet-zhang2023]; sugar umbrella review [adiet-huang2023].
- 2024: nut-fertility meta-analysis [adiet-cardoso2024]; sulforaphane autism multicentre trial [adiet-ou2024]; UK Biobank fish-oil and atrial fibrillation [adiet-chen2024]; TRE in metabolic syndrome [adiet-manoogian2024].
- 2025: Burden of Proof on processed meat, sugary drinks and trans fat [adiet-haile2025]; COSMOS cocoa and hsCRP [adiet-li2025]; isocaloric TRE trial [adiet-peters2025]; IF network and isocaloric meta-analyses [adiet-semnaniazad2025, adiet-hamsho2025]; Cochrane DASH review [adiet-bensaaud2025]; NHANES eating-window paper (the 2024 "91% higher CVD death" abstract) [adiet-chen2025].
- 2026: blueberry meta-analysis (vascular yes, cognition no) [adiet-he2026].

## Practical bottom line

- The broad pattern the site recommends (mostly plants, olive oil or other liquid vegetable oils instead of butter, nuts, legumes, fish, little processed meat or sugary drink) is the best-supported part of nutrition science, and CORDIOPREV strengthened it.
- Single-food headlines are weaker. Coffee, olive oil, whole grains and fruit and vegetables all look good in cohorts, but the size of their benefit is uncertain and probably smaller than the observational numbers suggest.
- Blood-pressure effects of individual foods (flaxseed, nitrate greens) are about 2-3 mmHg on average, not 10. DASH as a whole diet is the reliable blood-pressure tool.
- Fasting schedules work by cutting calories. Pick the one you can sustain.
- Do not drink red wine for health. Do not take high-dose fish oil without a reason; eat fish instead.
- For fertility, diet evidence is still mostly observational. Nuts (60-75 g/day) and a Mediterranean pattern have small trials behind them for sperm parameters; nothing yet for live birth.

## What we don't know

Whether any single food changes hard outcomes in healthy people independent of the overall diet; whether diet MR can be made reliable given weak, education-linked instruments; and whether fertility-relevant diet changes affect live birth rather than semen or IVF surrogates.
