# The oxidative theory of aging & 'longevity' supplements

Deep-dive area: `longevity`. Reference ids use the prefix `lon-`. Every reference was checked against its PubMed record (title, year, DOI, publication type and retraction status) during this research session. Two FDA documents without PMIDs were checked on the official FDA and regulations.gov sites. Cross-references to other factors on the site use the factor's id in square brackets.

---

## Key takeaways

1. **The simple free-radical theory of aging has failed its direct tests.** Harman proposed in 1956 that aging is caused by accumulated free-radical damage [lon-harman1956]. In mice, though, removing or adding antioxidant enzymes usually changed oxidative damage without changing lifespan [lon-vanremmen2003, lon-zhang2009, lon-perez2009b, lon-perez2009a]. Naked mole-rats live more than 28 years while carrying more oxidative damage than mice [lon-andziak2006, lon-perez2009c]. Antioxidant supplements did not lower human mortality [lon-bjelakovic2012].
2. **Oxidative damage still matters in specific places.** Catalase aimed at mitochondria extended mouse lifespan by about 5 months and reduced heart aging and cancer burden [lon-schriner2005, lon-dai2009, lon-treuting2008]. The current view is that oxidants are signals at low levels and one source of damage among many at high levels [lon-sies2017, lon-gladyshev2016].
3. **Small rises in mitochondrial oxidants can be healthy ("mitohormesis").** This helps explain why exercise is good for you and why antioxidant pills taken around training can cancel some of its benefit [lon-schulz2007, lon-ristow2009, lon-ristow2014].
4. **Among popular "longevity" supplements, only creatine has strong human evidence, and that evidence is for muscle when combined with training, not for slowing aging** [lon-chilibeck2017]. Taurine, glycine, carnosine and alpha-lipoic acid have small, specific effects at best. NAD+ precursors, astaxanthin, PQQ, ergothioneine and melatonin (as an antioxidant) have no proven clinical benefit in humans.
5. **Some popular options carry real risks.** Methylene blue can cause serotonin toxicity with common antidepressants [lon-gillman2011]. Rapamycin is an immunosuppressant whose only year-long healthy-adult trial missed its primary endpoint [lon-moel2025]. Melatonin products often contain very different amounts from their labels [lon-erland2017, lon-cohen2023].

## Part 1. The free-radical theory of aging and how it was revised

### 1.1 Harman's idea and its rise

In 1956 Denham Harman suggested that aging results from damage by free radicals made during normal oxygen metabolism, the same chemistry seen in radiation injury. He also suggested that antioxidants might slow it [lon-harman1956]. In 1972 he narrowed the idea: mitochondria, which make most of a cell's oxidants, would be both the source and the main victim, and their decline would set lifespan [lon-harman1972]. The "mitochondrial free-radical theory of aging" became the dominant explanation of aging through the 1990s. It is the intellectual root of the antioxidant-supplement industry [lon-speakman2011].

The theory made testable predictions. Animals with weaker antioxidant defences should age faster. Animals with stronger defences should live longer. Long-lived species should have less oxidative damage. Antioxidant supplements should extend life.

### 1.2 The mouse tests: change the enzymes, measure lifespan

Arlan Richardson and Holly Van Remmen's group in San Antonio ran the most systematic test. They engineered mice that lacked or overexpressed the main antioxidant enzymes and followed them for their whole lives.

| Mouse model | Oxidative damage | Cancer | Lifespan | Ref |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sod2+/- (50% less mitochondrial SOD) | Higher DNA oxidation (8-oxodG) in all tissues | Doubled tumour incidence | **Identical** (mean and maximum) | [lon-vanremmen2003] |
| Sod2+/- plus Gpx1-/- | Higher DNA and protein oxidation | More cancer | **No reduction** | [lon-zhang2009] |
| Overexpressing CuZnSOD, catalase, or both; CuZnSOD plus MnSOD | Lower | n/a | **Not extended** | [lon-perez2009b] |
| Sod1-/- (no cytosolic SOD) | Widespread, from 3 months | More than 70% got liver nodules or cancer | **Shorter** | [lon-elchuri2005] |
| Catalase targeted to mitochondria (MCAT) | Lower | Less non-blood cancer | **About 5 months longer** | [lon-schriner2005, lon-treuting2008] |

Summing up 18 such manipulations, the group found only one (Sod1 deletion) that changed lifespan, and it shortened it, largely through liver cancer [lon-perez2009a]. Worm studies agreed. Deleting any of the five superoxide dismutase genes in *C. elegans* did not shorten life, and deleting mitochondrial *sod-2* lengthened it despite more protein oxidation [lon-vanraamsdonk2009, lon-gems2009].

The pattern that emerged: oxidative damage clearly drives some diseases of aging, especially cancer, but in most models it did not set the pace of aging itself [lon-speakman2011, lon-lapointe2010].

### 1.3 Long-lived animals with lots of damage

If oxidative damage set lifespan, long-lived species should have less of it. The naked mole-rat is a mouse-sized rodent that lives more than 28 years, compared with about 3.5 years for a lab mouse. Rochelle Buffenstein's group found that even young mole-rats had at least twice the lipid peroxidation of mice [lon-andziak2006]. They also had more protein and DNA oxidation. What they had instead was more stable proteins and stronger protein-recycling (proteasome) activity [lon-perez2009c]. Mole-rats appear to tolerate damage rather than prevent it [lon-lewis2013]. Comparative studies across many species do tend to show that long-lived animals make fewer mitochondrial oxidants and have oxidation-resistant membranes, but correlation across species cannot prove cause [lon-shields2021, lon-barja2013].

### 1.4 Mitochondrial mutations without extra oxidants

The theory predicted a "vicious cycle": oxidants damage mitochondrial DNA, damaged mitochondria leak more oxidants, and so on. Mice engineered with an error-prone mitochondrial DNA polymerase pile up mitochondrial DNA mutations and age prematurely. But their mitochondria did not make more oxidants, and their cells were not more sensitive to oxidative stress [lon-trifunovic2005]. Mitochondrial decline can drive aging-like changes without going through oxidative damage.

### 1.5 Antioxidant supplements in humans

The human version of the experiment is the antioxidant supplement trial. A Cochrane review of 78 RCTs with 296,707 participants found that antioxidant supplements did not lower mortality. In low-bias trials, beta-carotene, vitamin E and higher-dose vitamin A slightly raised it [lon-bjelakovic2012]. This is covered in depth on [high-dose-antioxidant-cocktail], [vitamin-e-supplement] and [beta-carotene-supplement].

### 1.6 Mitohormesis: when a little oxidant stress is good

Michael Ristow's group turned the theory on its head. In worms, restricting glucose made mitochondria burn more fuel and produce more oxidants. That rise induced catalase and stress resistance and extended lifespan. Feeding the worms antioxidants abolished the extension [lon-schulz2007]. In young men, vitamin C (1000 mg/day) plus vitamin E (400 IU/day) blocked the rise in insulin sensitivity and in the body's own antioxidant enzymes that four weeks of training normally produces [lon-ristow2009]. Ristow named this "mitohormesis": a brief rise in mitochondrial oxidants acts as a signal that triggers lasting defences [lon-ristow2010, lon-ristow2014]. Finkel's group described the same pattern more broadly: mild mitochondrial stress switches on signals to the nucleus that make cells tougher [lon-yun2014]. See [antioxidant-megadoses-during-training] for the practical side.

### 1.7 The reframing: redox signalling, eustress and the deleteriome

Helmut Sies reframed oxidative stress in two parts. At about 1-10 nanomolar, hydrogen peroxide is a normal messenger that switches specific proteins on and off ("oxidative eustress"). At much higher levels it damages molecules ("oxidative distress") [lon-sies2017]. A 2022 expert statement went further and asked researchers to stop using the catch-all term "ROS", because hydrogen peroxide, superoxide and the hydroxyl radical behave so differently [lon-sies2022].

Vadim Gladyshev argued that "the free radical theory of aging is dead", and that oxidative damage is only one of countless by-products of imperfect biology [lon-gladyshev2014]. He calls the sum of all these accumulating harms the "deleteriome". In this view, aging is the rising deleteriome, shaped by genes, environment and chance [lon-gladyshev2016]. The current "hallmarks of aging" framework lists 12 hallmarks, including mitochondrial dysfunction, genomic instability and chronic inflammation. It treats oxidative damage as one input to these processes rather than as the single cause [lon-lopezotin2023].

### 1.8 What survives of the theory

The theory has serious defenders. Gustavo Barja argues that an updated mitochondrial version still explains why long-lived species make fewer oxidants at complex I and have oxidation-resistant membranes. He says many "refutations" rest on misunderstandings, such as measuring the wrong compartment [lon-barja2013]. Kirkwood and Kowald modelled where oxidants are made relative to their targets. They concluded that many negative experiments may be misleading, and that the theory survives as one of several mechanisms of aging [lon-kirkwood2012]. Liochev warns against overcorrecting, saying that treating ROS mainly as helpful signals ignores their well-documented direct damage [lon-liochev2013].

The best experimental support is mitochondria-targeted catalase. MCAT mice lived about 5 months longer, with less oxidative damage, fewer mitochondrial DNA deletions, delayed heart pathology and cataracts [lon-schriner2005]. Their hearts aged more slowly [lon-dai2009], and they died with less cancer burden [lon-treuting2008]. The lesson is that **where** an antioxidant acts matters. Mitochondrial hydrogen peroxide removed at its source had effects that body-wide SOD overexpression did not. This is the rationale for mitochondria-targeted drugs such as [mitoq], although swallowed molecules are a long way from a genetically built-in enzyme.

**Bottom line for Part 1.** Oxidative damage contributes to specific age-related diseases (especially cancer and heart aging), but it is not the master clock of aging. Low-level oxidants are needed signals, and blanket antioxidant supplementation is not a credible anti-aging strategy.

## Part 2. Popular "longevity" supplements, judged on human evidence

### 2.1 Taurine

**The claim.** In 2023, Singh and colleagues reported in *Science* that blood taurine falls with age in mice, monkeys and humans. Taurine supplements extended lifespan and health span in mice and improved health-span markers in middle-aged monkeys. In human cohorts, lower taurine was linked to several age-related diseases [lon-singh2023]. An accompanying commentary called for human trials [lon-mcgaunn2023].

**The critique.** In 2025 an NIH group (Fernandez, Ferrucci, de Cabo) measured taurine repeatedly in the same people across three human cohorts, and also in monkeys and mice. Taurine went **up or stayed flat** with age. Its links to physical function and metabolism were inconsistent [lon-fernandez2025]. The "taurine deficiency drives aging" idea now rests mainly on animal supplementation data.

**Human trials.** There are no aging-outcome trials. A meta-analysis of 25 RCTs (1,024 people, 0.5-6 g/day) found modest drops in systolic blood pressure (about 4 mmHg), diastolic pressure (about 1.5 mmHg), fasting glucose (about 6 mg/dL) and triglycerides (about 18 mg/dL), with no excess side effects [lon-tzang2024]. In the best single trial, 1.6 g/day for 12 weeks lowered clinic systolic pressure by 7.2 mmHg versus 2.6 mmHg on placebo in people with prehypertension, and improved blood-vessel function [lon-sun2016]. Taurine also slightly improves endurance performance [lon-waldron2018]. A risk assessment set 3 g/day as an observed safe level for long-term use [lon-shao2008].

**Verdict.** 1.5-3 g/day is safe and may nudge blood pressure and metabolic markers. Taking it to slow aging is speculation.

### 2.2 Creatine

**Muscle.** Creatine is the best-studied supplement in this list. In older adults doing resistance training, creatine added about 1.4 kg of lean mass and improved chest- and leg-press strength more than training alone. This held across 22 RCTs with 721 participants [lon-chilibeck2017], consistent with an earlier meta-analysis [lon-devries2014]. The usual dose is 3-5 g/day. An optional loading phase (about 20 g/day for 5-7 days) fills muscle stores faster [lon-kreider2017].

**Brain.** Meta-analyses report small memory gains. One found an overall effect size of 0.29, driven by older adults (0.88), with no effect in people aged 11-31 [lon-prokopidis2023]. Another found 0.31 across 16 RCTs [lon-xu2024]. A letter argued that the first analysis double-counted data and may be a false positive [lon-eckert2023]. An earlier systematic review found mixed results and a better response in vegetarians [lon-avgerinos2018]. A single large dose (0.35 g/kg) partly offset the cognitive effects of a night of sleep deprivation in a small study [lon-gordjinejad2024].

**Antioxidant claims.** Cell and animal studies suggest creatine has direct and indirect antioxidant effects [lon-sestili2011]. No human study shows that this matters clinically. Hopes for neuroprotection were tested properly and failed. Creatine 10 g/day for at least 5 years did not slow Parkinson disease in 1,741 patients [lon-kieburtz2015]. Up to 40 g/day did not slow Huntington disease [lon-hersch2017].

**Safety.** In healthy people, creatine does not damage the kidneys [lon-desouzaesilva2019]. It raises blood creatinine slightly because creatinine is its breakdown product, which can be mistaken for kidney trouble on a lab report. Dehydration, cramps and hair loss are not supported by trial evidence [lon-antonio2021]. People with existing kidney disease should involve their doctor.

**Fertility.** Creatine supports energy use in reproductive tissues and the placenta. In spiny mice, giving creatine during pregnancy protected newborns from birth-asphyxia injury to the brain, kidney and muscle [lon-muccini2021, lon-larosa2016]. Human pregnancy and fertility data are lacking [lon-muccini2021]. A 15-man pilot found only a non-significant trend toward higher sperm concentration [lon-nedeljkovic2026].

**Verdict.** 3-5 g/day is cheap, safe and effective for muscle when combined with strength training, especially in older adults. It is not a proven brain or longevity supplement.

### 2.3 Glycine and GlyNAC

**Glycine for sleep.** 3 g of glycine before bed reduced next-day fatigue and improved vigilance during three nights of restricted sleep [lon-bannai2012]. In rats it works by widening skin blood vessels and lowering core temperature via the brain's clock centre [lon-kawai2015]. The human studies are small and from one research group.

**Glycine and lifespan.** In the NIA Interventions Testing Program (ITP), which tests compounds at three sites in genetically mixed mice, an 8% glycine diet extended lifespan by a modest 4-6% in both sexes [lon-miller2019].

**GlyNAC.** Glutathione is made from cysteine, glutamate and glycine. Older adults had about half the red-cell glutathione of young adults, and 2 weeks of cysteine (as NAC) plus glycine restored it and lowered F2-isoprostanes [lon-sekhar2011]. The same Baylor group then reported that 24 weeks of GlyNAC improved strength, gait speed, cognition and many biomarkers in 8 older adults (open-label) [lon-kumar2021]. A 16-week placebo-controlled RCT in 24 older adults found similar improvements [lon-kumar2023]. GlyNAC-fed mice lived 24% longer in a single-lab study [lon-kumar2022].

**The critique.** A Nestle-run RCT in 114 healthy older adults gave GlyNAC at 2.4, 4.8 or 7.2 g/day for 2 weeks. It did **not** raise glutathione or the reduced-to-oxidized glutathione ratio, which was the primary endpoint. Only a post-hoc subgroup with high oxidative stress and low glutathione responded [lon-lizzo2022]. The Nestle doses were lower and shorter than Baylor's (about 16 g/day for 16-24 weeks), so the trials do not directly contradict each other. Still, the positive results come from very small trials by one group that measured dozens of outcomes. Nestle Health Science holds patents and licences on this use [lon-lizzo2022].

**Verdict.** Glycine 3 g at night is a reasonable, low-risk sleep experiment. GlyNAC may help older adults who are actually glutathione-deficient, but this needs independent replication with clinical outcomes. See also [n-acetylcysteine].

### 2.4 NAD+ precursors (NMN and NR)

**What they do.** NAD+ is essential for mitochondrial energy production and is used up by sirtuins and the DNA-repair enzymes PARPs. It falls with age in rodent tissues. Nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) are vitamin B3 forms that raise NAD+. NR at 100, 300 and 1000 mg/day raised whole-blood NAD+ by 22%, 51% and 142% within 2 weeks, without excess side effects [lon-conze2019, lon-trammell2016]. NMN at 250-900 mg/day also raised blood NAD+ [lon-irie2020, lon-igarashi2022, lon-yi2023].

**Do the rises do anything?**

| Trial | Population | Dose | Main result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yoshino 2021 [lon-yoshino2021] | 25 prediabetic women | NMN 250 mg/day, 10 wk | Muscle insulin sensitivity **improved** |
| Dollerup 2018 [lon-dollerup2018] | 40 obese men | NR 2 g/day, 12 wk | Insulin sensitivity **unchanged** |
| Dollerup 2020 [lon-dollerup2020] | Same trial | NR 2 g/day | Muscle mitochondria and muscle NAD+ **unchanged** |
| Remie 2020 [lon-remie2020] | 13 overweight adults | NR 1 g/day, 6 wk | Insulin sensitivity and mitochondrial function **unchanged**; slight rise in fat-free mass |
| Elhassan 2019 [lon-elhassan2019] | 12 older men | NR 1 g/day, 3 wk | Muscle NAD+ metabolites up, inflammatory cytokines down; mitochondrial function **unchanged** |
| Martens 2018 [lon-martens2018] | 30 middle-aged and older adults | NR 1 g/day, 6 wk | Safe; possible small blood-pressure signal |
| Yi 2023 [lon-yi2023] | 80 middle-aged adults | NMN 300-900 mg/day, 60 days | Longer 6-minute walk; HOMA-IR **unchanged** |
| Brakedal 2022 [lon-brakedal2022] | 30 with Parkinson disease | NR 1 g/day, 30 days | Brain NAD+ up in some; mild improvement in responders (phase I) |
| Meta-analysis 2025 [lon-prokopidis2025] | Adults over 60 | NMN or NR | **No benefit** for muscle mass, grip or gait speed |

The one clearly positive metabolic trial was criticized because the NMN group started with much less liver fat (6.3% versus 14.8%), so randomization may have failed [lon-brenner2021]. In mice, NR did not extend lifespan in the ITP [lon-harrison2021]. Reviews agree that these compounds are safe in the short term and raise NAD+, but that small, varied trials have not shown physiological benefit [lon-freeberg2023]. Several of the key pharmacokinetic and safety trials were sponsored by the NR manufacturer [lon-trammell2016, lon-conze2019].

**Regulatory status.** In 2022 FDA said NMN was excluded from the US definition of a dietary supplement, because it had first been studied as a drug. On 29 September 2025, responding to a petition from the Natural Products Association and the Alliance for Natural Health USA, FDA changed its interpretation and determined that NMN is not excluded [lon-fda2025nmn]. New-dietary-ingredient notifications for NMN have since been filed with FDA [lon-fdandi].

**Verdict.** NR and NMN reliably raise a blood number. They have not reliably improved anything people care about. The egg-quality evidence (mouse only) is on the same page.

### 2.5 Melatonin as a general antioxidant

**The claim.** Russel Reiter and colleagues describe melatonin as a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant, made inside mitochondria, that scavenges radicals and boosts antioxidant enzymes [lon-reiter2017]. This rests mostly on cell and animal work, often at concentrations far above those reached with sleep doses.

**Human antioxidant evidence.** A meta-analysis of 15 RCTs found melatonin raised "total antioxidant capacity" and lowered MDA and protein carbonyls [lon-ghorbaninejad2020]. These were mostly patient groups, the results varied a lot between studies, and the markers are weak ones (see the biomarker section of the foundations page). No trial shows that melatonin slows aging or prevents disease through an antioxidant effect.

**As a sleep aid.** Melatonin shortened sleep onset by about 7 minutes and lengthened sleep by about 8 minutes in a meta-analysis of 19 trials [lon-ferracioliOda2013]. A dose-response meta-analysis found effects rising up to about 4 mg/day, and taking it about 3 hours before bedtime worked better than right before [lon-cruzsanabria2024]. Lower doses can work: 0.3 mg restored sleep efficiency in insomniacs over 50, while 3 mg caused a drop in body temperature and left melatonin levels high into the morning [lon-zhdanova2001]. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine suggests not using melatonin for chronic insomnia in adults [lon-sateia2017]. It is more useful for timing problems such as jet lag and [shift-work-circadian].

**What is in the bottle.** In 31 supplement products, melatonin content ranged from 83% below to 478% above the label, and 8 contained serotonin [lon-erland2017]. In 25 US gummy products, content ranged from 74% to 347% of the label. Only 3 were within 10%, and one contained undeclared CBD and no melatonin at all [lon-cohen2023]. US pediatric melatonin ingestions reported to poison centres rose 530% between 2012 and 2021. Five children needed mechanical ventilation and two died [lon-lelak2022].

**Safety.** Short-term adverse events are generally mild, such as daytime sleepiness, headache and dizziness [lon-besag2019]. Placebo-controlled long-term data are scarce [lon-besag2022].

**Verdict.** Melatonin is a modest timing and sleep-onset aid at 0.3-5 mg. There is no good evidence that it works as an anti-aging antioxidant. Product quality is a real problem. The egg-quality (IVF) evidence is on the same page.

### 2.6 MitoQ and SkQ1 (mitochondria-targeted antioxidants)

These attach an antioxidant (a ubiquinone for MitoQ, a plastoquinone for SkQ1) to a positively charged carrier that piles up inside mitochondria [lon-murphy2018].

**Positive results.** MitoQ 20 mg/day for 6 weeks improved flow-mediated dilation by 42% in 20 older adults [lon-rossman2018]. A 4-week pilot in 18 people with chronic kidney disease found a similar improvement, but no change in exercise capacity [lon-kirkman2023]. Three weeks of MitoQ reduced exercise-induced DNA damage in muscle and blood cells, while a single dose did nothing [lon-williamson2020]. In hepatitis C, liver enzymes fell from baseline, though not significantly versus placebo [lon-gane2010].

**Null or negative results.** MitoQ 40-80 mg/day for 12 months did not slow Parkinson disease in 128 patients [lon-snow2010]. In middle-aged men, MitoQ mildly lowered mitochondrial hydrogen peroxide but did not change mitochondrial function or urinary F2-isoprostanes [lon-pham2020]. After muscle-damaging exercise, MitoQ did not reduce soreness, delayed recovery of strength and raised creatine kinase [lon-broome2022]. This is consistent with mitohormesis. A single 100-160 mg dose did not harm the kidneys [lon-linder2024].

**SkQ1.** SkQ1 extended lifespan in some rodent strains, with effects strongly dependent on housing and pathogen exposure [lon-anisimov2011]. Its only human trials are company-funded eye-drop studies for dry eye [lon-petrov2016, lon-brzheskiy2015].

**Verdict.** Interesting and better targeted than vitamin pills, but still experimental. The evidence is not enough to recommend for healthy people.

### 2.7 Astaxanthin

A red carotenoid from algae, found in salmon and shrimp. A meta-analysis of 12 small RCTs found it lowered blood MDA and, in type 2 diabetes, IL-6 [lon-ma2022]. With exercise training it may improve performance, but effects on cognition were null or marginal [lon-liu2024]. In the ITP it extended median lifespan by 12% in male mice but not in females [lon-harrison2024]. Ames listed it as a putative "longevity vitamin" [lon-ames2018]. On safety, 87 human studies of natural astaxanthin raised no concerns. EFSA's lower 2 mg/day acceptable intake was based on a rat study of synthetic astaxanthin [lon-brendler2019].

**Verdict.** Safe at usual doses and intriguing in male mice, but with no human clinical benefit shown. The sperm-specific evidence (one small trial) is on the same page.

### 2.8 Alpha-lipoic acid (ALA)

ALA is a mitochondrial enzyme cofactor that, as a supplement, acts as a thiol antioxidant and metal chelator. Its real use is diabetic neuropathy. In SYDNEY 2, oral ALA 600 mg/day for 5 weeks halved symptom scores, compared with a 32% fall on placebo. Higher doses added nausea and vomiting without more benefit [lon-ziegler2006]. A meta-analysis supported IV use but questioned the clinical relevance of oral dosing [lon-mijnhout2012]. The 4-year NATHAN 1 trial (600 mg/day) missed its primary endpoint, improved some secondary scores, and had more serious adverse events on ALA (38% versus 28%) [lon-ziegler2011]. For weight loss, the effect is about 1.3 kg [lon-kucukgoncu2017]. A rare but serious risk is insulin autoimmune syndrome. Six European patients developed severe low blood sugar 30-120 days after starting 600 mg/day, all carrying particular HLA types [lon-gullo2014].

**Verdict.** Reasonable under medical guidance for neuropathy symptoms. Not a general longevity supplement. The egg-quality evidence is on the same page.

### 2.9 PQQ

Pyrroloquinoline quinone is marketed for "mitochondrial biogenesis". In 23 untrained men, PQQ 20 mg/day during 6 weeks of training raised muscle PGC-1alpha but did not improve aerobic performance beyond training alone [lon-hwang2020]. A 10-person study found short-term drops in CRP and IL-6 [lon-harris2013]. Two small Japanese trials of branded products reported better scores on some cognitive tests [lon-itoh2016, lon-shiojima2022]. EFSA judged up to 20 mg/day safe for healthy adults (not in pregnancy), but did not assess benefit [lon-efsa2017pqq]. Ames proposed it as a longevity vitamin, a hypothesis rather than evidence [lon-ames2018].

**Verdict.** Unproven. Exercise is a far more powerful stimulus for new mitochondria; see [aerobic-exercise-moderate].

### 2.10 Carnosine and anserine

These are dipeptides found in meat and fish. In the lab they buffer acid, bind metals and block glycation [lon-boldyrev2013]. In 30 overweight adults, carnosine 2 g/day for 12 weeks prevented the rise in insulin resistance seen on placebo [lon-decourten2016]. Meta-analyses of these dipeptides report lower HbA1c (about -0.8%) and waist size (about -3.5 cm) [lon-menon2020]. Another meta-analysis found glycaemic benefits but could include only four human studies [lon-matthews2021]. In 30 people with mild cognitive impairment, anserine 500 mg/day for 12 weeks slightly improved MMSE scores compared with placebo [lon-masuoka2021].

**Verdict.** A possible small glycaemic benefit that needs larger trials. Not an established longevity aid.

### 2.11 Ergothioneine (mushrooms)

Ergothioneine is made only by fungi and some bacteria. Humans have a dedicated transporter (OCTN1) that concentrates it in blood and tissues [lon-grundemann2005, lon-halliwell2018]. Blood levels fall after age 60 and are lower in mild cognitive impairment [lon-cheah2016]. In 3,236 Swedish adults followed for 21 years, each standard-deviation higher plasma ergothioneine was linked to 14% lower overall mortality and 21% lower cardiovascular mortality [lon-smith2020]. The authors noted it was the strongest marker of a health-conscious diet, so confounding is likely. Across cohorts, eating mushrooms was linked to slightly lower mortality (RR 0.94) [lon-ba2021]. Pure ergothioneine is absorbed and retained, but oxidative-damage markers showed only non-significant downward trends [lon-cheah2017]. A 19-person, one-year pilot in mild cognitive impairment reported better verbal learning [lon-yau2024]. EFSA considers synthetic ergothioneine safe, including in pregnancy [lon-efsa2017erg].

**Verdict.** Eating mushrooms regularly is a sensible food choice. Ergothioneine supplements are unproven.

### 2.12 Methylene blue

**The hype.** Methylene blue is a century-old dye and drug, now promoted online as a mitochondrial "energy" and longevity enhancer. In animals, low doses shuttle electrons in the respiratory chain and improve memory [lon-rojas2012]. In 26 healthy adults, one low dose increased brain activity on fMRI during attention and memory tasks, and correct memory answers rose 7% [lon-rodriguez2016]. In bipolar disorder, 195 mg/day added to lamotrigine improved residual depression and anxiety [lon-alda2017].

**The evidence against.** In the ITP, methylene blue did not extend median lifespan in mice [lon-harrison2014]. A stabilized derivative (LMTM) failed its 891-patient phase 3 Alzheimer trial [lon-gauthier2016]. The sponsor later argued that its low-dose "control" was itself active [lon-vanmaanen2026], a conflicted, post-hoc explanation.

**The danger.** Methylene blue is a potent inhibitor of MAO-A, the enzyme that breaks down serotonin [lon-ramsay2007]. Of 14 reported cases of brain toxicity after methylene blue, 13 met criteria for serotonin toxicity. This occurred at doses as low as 1 mg/kg in people taking serotonin reuptake inhibitors, the most common antidepressants. Every proposed dose reaches MAO-A-blocking levels [lon-gillman2011].

**Verdict.** Avoid as a self-directed supplement, and never combine it with antidepressants without medical supervision.

### 2.13 Rapamycin (brief)

Rapamycin inhibits mTORC1, a nutrient-sensing switch. It is the most reproducible drug for extending mouse lifespan, and it works even when started late in life [lon-harrison2009]. In older adults, low-dose mTOR inhibitors for 6 weeks improved vaccine responses by about 20% and reduced reported infections [lon-mannick2014, lon-mannick2018]. PEARL, a 48-week RCT of 5 or 10 mg compounded rapamycin weekly in healthy, normally ageing adults, found it relatively safe. But visceral fat, its primary outcome, did not change. Lean mass and pain improved only in women on 10 mg [lon-moel2025]. A survey of 333 off-label users reported few problems, but self-reported data from people with commercial ties to longevity medicine cannot establish safety or benefit [lon-kaeberlein2023]. Known risks include more infections and higher cholesterol and triglycerides in patients [lon-lee2024], and insulin resistance through mTORC2 disruption, shown in mice [lon-lamming2012].

**Verdict.** Experimental. It should be used only under medical supervision, ideally in trials.

## Sperm and egg relevance

- **Melatonin, NMN/NR, alpha-lipoic acid and astaxanthin** have separate fertility entries on the same pages ([melatonin], [nmn-nad], [alpha-lipoic-acid], [astaxanthin]). The general-health verdicts here do not change those assessments. For eggs, NMN evidence is mouse-only. Melatonin has small IVF trials.
- **Creatine** supports energy in reproductive tissues and protected newborns in animal pregnancy models [lon-muccini2021, lon-larosa2016]. The only human male data are a 15-man pilot with a non-significant trend [lon-nedeljkovic2026]. There is no basis yet to use it for fertility.
- **Rapamycin and methylene blue** should not be used by anyone trying to conceive outside a medical setting. Rapamycin's reproductive effects were not assessed in the human aging studies [lon-lee2024], and none of the methylene blue studies cited here assessed fertility.
- **Mitohormesis** applies to fertility too: blanket antioxidant use is not automatically protective. See [high-dose-antioxidant-cocktail].

## Where the evidence is contested

1. **Is the free-radical theory dead?** Gladyshev, Lapointe and Hekimi, and Speakman and Selman argue it has failed its key tests [lon-gladyshev2014, lon-lapointe2010, lon-speakman2011], citing mouse genetics [lon-perez2009a] and naked mole-rats [lon-lewis2013]. Barja, Kirkwood and Liochev argue an updated version survives, and that many negative experiments targeted the wrong compartment [lon-barja2013, lon-kirkwood2012, lon-liochev2013]. Mitochondria-targeted catalase is the strongest evidence for the defenders [lon-schriner2005]. Most current reviews settle in the middle: oxidative damage contributes to specific diseases but is not the clock of aging [lon-shields2021, lon-gladyshev2016].
2. **Taurine as an aging driver.** Singh et al. found taurine falls with age and extends mouse life [lon-singh2023]. Fernandez et al. found human taurine does not decline with age in longitudinal data [lon-fernandez2025].
3. **NMN and insulin sensitivity.** The positive Yoshino trial [lon-yoshino2021] was challenged over a baseline liver-fat imbalance [lon-brenner2021]. Several NR trials were null [lon-dollerup2018, lon-remie2020], and a muscle meta-analysis was negative [lon-prokopidis2025].
4. **GlyNAC.** Baylor's small positive trials [lon-kumar2021, lon-kumar2023] contrast with Nestle's larger null 2-week RCT [lon-lizzo2022]. Both groups have commercial interests.
5. **Creatine and memory.** Positive meta-analyses [lon-prokopidis2023, lon-xu2024] contrast with a statistical critique [lon-eckert2023] and mixed earlier reviews [lon-avgerinos2018].
6. **Melatonin's antioxidant role.** A mechanistic advocacy literature [lon-reiter2017] and biomarker meta-analysis [lon-ghorbaninejad2020] contrast with the absence of clinical outcome data and a guideline recommending against its use for chronic insomnia [lon-sateia2017].
7. **MitoQ.** Positive vascular pilots [lon-rossman2018, lon-kirkman2023] contrast with a null long Parkinson trial [lon-snow2010] and worse recovery after exercise [lon-broome2022].
8. **Methylene blue.** Mechanistic enthusiasm [lon-rojas2012] contrasts with null mouse lifespan and phase 3 results [lon-harrison2014, lon-gauthier2016] and a clear toxicity mechanism [lon-gillman2011].
9. **Rapamycin.** Robust mouse data [lon-harrison2009] contrast with a missed human primary endpoint [lon-moel2025] and safety claims based on uncontrolled surveys [lon-kaeberlein2023].
10. **Astaxanthin safety limits.** EFSA's 2 mg/day ADI contrasts with a review arguing natural astaxanthin is safe at 12 mg/day or more [lon-brendler2019].

## Practical bottom line

- **Things that actually slow age-related decline** are not on this list. They are regular exercise ([aerobic-exercise-moderate], [resistance-training]), adequate sleep ([short-sleep]), not smoking, and keeping body fat and blood sugar in check ([glycemic-control]). Mitohormesis explains part of why exercise works, and why high-dose antioxidant pills around training can undercut it.
- **If you want one supplement with solid evidence:** creatine monohydrate 3-5 g/day, combined with strength training, especially after about 50 [lon-chilibeck2017, lon-kreider2017].
- **Reasonable low-risk experiments:** taurine 1.5-3 g/day for blood pressure [lon-tzang2024, lon-shao2008]. Glycine 3 g at bedtime for sleep [lon-bannai2012]. Melatonin 0.3-3 mg for jet lag or sleep timing, choosing a third-party-tested product [lon-zhdanova2001, lon-cohen2023]. Mushrooms in the diet [lon-ba2021].
- **Not worth paying for (for health outcomes):** NMN/NR, PQQ, astaxanthin, ergothioneine supplements, and MitoQ for healthy people.
- **Avoid without medical supervision:** methylene blue (serotonin toxicity) and rapamycin (immunosuppression, metabolic effects) [lon-gillman2011, lon-lee2024].
- **Alpha-lipoic acid** belongs under a doctor's care for neuropathy. Stop it and get tested if you have unexplained low blood sugar [lon-gullo2014].

---

## What we don't know

- Whether any supplement here changes human aging rate, disease incidence or lifespan. No trial has measured these outcomes.
- Whether people with measurably low glutathione, NAD+ or taurine benefit more, as the "replete people don't benefit" pattern from antioxidant trials would predict [lon-bjelakovic2012, lon-lizzo2022].
- Whether mitochondria-targeted antioxidants, unlike general antioxidants, can lower damage without blocking useful signals in humans [lon-broome2022, lon-schriner2005].
- Long-term safety of multi-year use of NMN/NR, GlyNAC, high-dose melatonin and intermittent rapamycin [lon-freeberg2023, lon-besag2022, lon-moel2025].
- Whether the sex-specific mouse lifespan effects (astaxanthin in males only) mean anything for people [lon-harrison2024].
- How to measure biological aging reliably enough to run shorter trials [lon-lopezotin2023].
