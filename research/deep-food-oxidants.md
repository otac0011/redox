# The oxidant load of food itself: AGEs, oxidised fats, heme and cooking by-products

Most of Redox Compass is about what food adds to your defences: polyphenols, sulforaphane, vitamin C. This page is about the other side of the plate. Some foods carry oxidants, or the raw material for making them, into the gut. The main candidates are browning products (advanced glycation end products, or AGEs), oxidised fats (malondialdehyde, 4-hydroxynonenal and oxidised cholesterol), heme iron from red meat, nitrite and N-nitroso compounds in processed meat, cooking by-products (heterocyclic amines, PAHs and acrylamide), a large fat load in one meal, alcohol, and a few contaminant metals.

The purpose is practical. The site wants to show, for every food and recipe, how much oxidative stress the food itself brings. This page builds the evidence base for that "Oxidant load" score and weighs each component by how strong the human evidence is, not by how alarming the chemistry sounds. The proposed scoring rules are in a separate rubric file, and the numbers behind them (AGE values, heme per serving, oxysterol, HCA, PAH and acrylamide contents) are in a data table for the scoring agents.

**The short version**

- **Strongest human evidence:** alcohol (randomised trials show dose-dependent rises in isoprostanes [fox-meagher1999, fox-schrieks2013]) and processed meat (a Group 1 carcinogen [fox-bouvard2015], with faecal N-nitroso and lipid-oxidation markers rising in feeding trials [fox-joosen2009, fox-pierre2013]).
- **Moderate:** heme iron from red meat in the gut [fox-pierre2006, fox-bingham2002, fox-bastide2011], high-heat cooking of meat (HCAs and PAHs [fox-martinezgongora2019]), and large high-fat meals [fox-fewkes2022, fox-bloomer2010].
- **Limited:** dietary oxidised fats, which are absorbed after a meal [fox-gorelik2008a, fox-staprans1994] but have no outcome data, and acrylamide, whose human cancer data are mostly null.
- **Weak and contested:** dietary AGEs. The trials conflict, the cohorts are null, and the two food databases rank foods in nearly opposite orders [fox-uribarri2010, fox-scheijen2016, fox-hellwig2024].
- **Meal composition matters:** herbs, spices, coffee, fruit and vegetables eaten with meat cut the post-meal rise in oxidised fat by 50-100% in small trials [fox-li2010, fox-sirota2013, fox-gorelik2008a].

## 1. Dietary AGEs

### What they are

AGEs form when sugars, or reactive sugar fragments such as glyoxal and methylglyoxal, react with the amino acids lysine and arginine. This is the same Maillard chemistry that browns toast and sears steak. The best-studied AGEs are carboxymethyl-lysine (CML), carboxyethyl-lysine (CEL) and the methylglyoxal hydroimidazolone MG-H1. The body also makes AGEs itself, especially when blood sugar is high.

Dry heat raises AGE content 10- to 100-fold over the uncooked food, while moist heat, shorter and cooler cooking, and acid (lemon juice or vinegar marinades halved AGE formation in beef) keep it down [fox-uribarri2010].

### Two databases that disagree

| | Uribarri et al. 2010 [fox-uribarri2010] | Scheijen et al. 2016 [fox-scheijen2016] |
|---|---|---|
| Method | ELISA: an antibody to CML, results in "kU" | UPLC-MS/MS: measured mass of CML, CEL and MG-H1 |
| Foods | 549 (US, many brand-name and by cooking method) | 190 (Dutch, common foods) |
| Highest | Fats and oils, butter (26,480 kU/100 g), cream cheese, broiled or fried meats, bacon (91,577 kU/100 g), roasted nuts, grated Parmesan | High-heat nut and grain products, canned meats, peanut butter-type spreads (MG-H1 204-445 mg/kg), breakfast cereals (MG-H1 up to 416 mg/kg), biscuits [fox-kataoka2025] |
| Lowest | Fruit, vegetables, milk, grains | Fruit, vegetables, **butter** and coffee |
| Strength | Many cooking-method contrasts within the same food | Validated, chemically specific, used by independent cohorts |

The disagreement is not subtle. Uribarri's table puts a teaspoon of butter or oil among the larger AGE sources. Scheijen's puts butter among the lowest-AGE foods. ELISA results depend on the food matrix: a GC-MS method agreed with ELISA in powdered infant formula but not liquid formula [fox-charissou2007], and reviewers note that no method yet covers all AGEs and that lab procedures have never been properly cross-validated [fox-ames2008, fox-poulsen2013].

**Which database to use.** For ranking foods against each other, mass spectrometry (Scheijen) is more defensible. It measures named compounds by mass, has published accuracy figures, and has been used by independent groups (the Maastricht Study, EPIC) to test health links. Fat-rich foods with almost no protein, such as butter and oils, cannot carry much protein-bound CML, which makes their very high ELISA values chemically implausible. The ELISA database is still useful for one thing: comparing cooking methods for the same food (poached chicken breast about 1,100 kU/100 g against broiled at 5,800 or deep-fried breaded at 9,700) [fox-uribarri2010]. The data table keeps both, with that rule stated.

### How much is absorbed

In the classic study, about 10% of the AGE immunoreactivity eaten in a single meal appeared in serum, and only about a third of that was excreted in urine [fox-koschinsky1997]. In adolescents, doubling dietary CML (5.4 to 11.3 mg/day) tripled CML in stool but did not raise urinary CML proportionally [fox-delgadoandrade2012]. Absorption differs by compound: about half of free pyrraline and 60% of free pentosidine (from coffee) reached the urine, but only 2% of protein-bound pentosidine from pretzels [fox-forster2005]. Higher habitual intake does show up as higher free CML, CEL and MG-H1 in plasma and urine [fox-scheijen2018], so some does get in.

### Do dietary AGEs raise oxidative stress in people?

**Supportive trials.** The Mount Sinai group (Vlassara and Uribarri) found that a 30-50% lower-AGE diet for 4 months lowered serum AGEs and oxidant-stress markers in healthy adults [fox-vlassara2009], that a 1-year low-AGE diet improved insulin resistance and oxidative stress in metabolic syndrome [fox-vlassara2016], and that intake tracked serum CML, which tracked 8-isoprostanes (r = 0.45) [fox-uribarri2007]. A high-AGE meal cut flow-mediated dilation by 36% versus 21% for the low-AGE version in diabetes [fox-negrean2007]. Two independent blinded trials using mass-spectrometry-verified diets found better insulin sensitivity on low-AGE diets after 2-4 weeks [fox-decourten2016, fox-mark2014]. Meta-analyses pool to lower HOMA-IR (about -1.2 to -1.3) and, in a few trials, lower 8-isoprostane and TNF-alpha [fox-baye2017, fox-sohouli2021, fox-clarke2016, fox-lu2025].

**Null or contrary.** A double-blind 4-week trial in 73 obese adults, with diets differing 2.7-, 5.3- and 3.7-fold in CML, CEL and MG-H1, found no change in clamp-measured insulin sensitivity, vascular function, inflammation or lipids [fox-linkens2022]. A 6-week trial found no effect on endothelial function or inflammation [fox-semba2014]. In over 6,000 people, habitual intake was unrelated to diabetes or microvascular function [fox-linkens2023, fox-linkens2022b]. Cohort meta-analyses found no link with cancer (HR 1.04) [fox-sharifizahabi2024] or mortality (HR 1.02) [fox-ahmadirad2025], apart from a possible invasive breast-cancer signal (HR 1.14) [fox-pascualmorena2025]. In EPIC's 450,000 people, more CML and MG-H1 went with slightly *less* colorectal cancer (HR 0.92), probably because cereals are the main source [fox-aglago2021]. Systematic reviewers rate the trial evidence low to very low [fox-kellow2013], and the German Research Foundation's food-safety commission concluded in 2024 that there is no convincing evidence of a causal link between dietary glycation compounds and harm [fox-hellwig2024].

**Verdict.** The best-blinded, best-measured trial was null and the largest cohorts are null. Low-AGE diets probably help some people mainly because they mean less frying and grilling and more stewing and vegetables. AGEs deserve a small weight in a food score, folded into the cooking-method points rather than scored on their own.

## 2. Oxidised fats: MDA, 4-HNE, oxysterols and frying oil

### The stomach as a bioreactor

Kanner's group showed that the acidic stomach accelerates fat oxidation catalysed by myoglobin and iron: heated muscle in simulated gastric fluid accumulated lipid hydroperoxides 6-fold, to 1,200 uM, in 2 hours, and catechin or red-wine polyphenols prevented it [fox-kanner2001]. In rats fed red turkey-meat cutlets, stomach hydroperoxides and malondialdehyde (MDA) built up and plasma MDA rose 50% [fox-gorelik2008b].

In people, a meal of 250 g of heated turkey cutlets raised plasma MDA by 160 nM from a baseline of 50 nM. Drinking red wine with the meal cut the rise by 75%, and soaking the cutlets in wine before cooking prevented it entirely [fox-gorelik2008a]. Coffee (200 mL roasted) cut the rise by 80% at 2 hours and 50% at 4 hours [fox-sirota2013]. An independent US group found that a polyphenol-rich spice blend cut MDA in cooked burger meat by 71%, prevented the plasma MDA rise and lowered urinary MDA by 49% [fox-li2010], and a Chilean group saw similar effects with berry concentrate and turkey burgers [fox-urquiaga2017]. The same Israeli group later ranked more than 50 plant foods by how well 100 g of each suppressed peroxidation of 200 g of meat in a gastric model; protection tracked polyphenol content (R2 = 0.75) [fox-kanner2017]. In that model, olive oil cut meat MDA (122 to 48 uM) while tuna or omega-3 oil raised it sharply (96 to 514 uM) [fox-tirosh2015].

### Absorbed oxidised fat and oxidised cholesterol

When volunteers ate highly oxidised corn oil, oxidised lipid in their chylomicrons rose 4.7-fold [fox-staprans1994]. A meal containing an oxidised cholesterol (alpha-epoxy-cholesterol) appeared in chylomicrons and then moved into LDL and HDL for up to 72 hours [fox-staprans2003]. A salami-and-Parmesan meal raised plasma 7-hydroxy- and 7-ketocholesterol, peaking at 8 hours [fox-linseisen1998]. Cholesterol oxidation products are cytotoxic and pro-inflammatory in experiments [fox-otaeguiarrazola2010], and Kanner argues that repeated intake of these "advanced lipid oxidation end-products" is a chronic threat [fox-kanner2007].

**Where oxidised cholesterol is concentrated** [fox-savage2002, fox-khan2015, fox-chudy2021]:

| Food | Cholesterol oxides |
|---|---|
| Fresh egg yolk, fresh meat, fresh soft or hard cheese | traces |
| Spray-dried egg yolk powder | 55-113 mg/100 g |
| Traditional ghee (butter heated about 150 C for 20-25 min) | about 12% of its cholesterol |
| Grated aged cheeses (Parmesan, Romano) | 6-32 ug/g in one cited range |
| Dried anchovy | 127 ug/g |
| Beef hamburger: fresh, fried, then 2 weeks refrigerated | 8.4, 29.4, 49.5 ug/g |
| Processed meats after cooking | 0-0.73% of cholesterol; up to 3.3% after storage and microwave reheating |
| Milk powder after 24 months' storage | 1.81% of cholesterol |

The pattern: oxidised cholesterol builds up with drying, powdering, long storage and reheating of cooked animal foods. Fresh foods cooked once contain little. Analytical methods are poorly harmonised, so treat these as orders of magnitude.

### Frying oil

Frying oil is covered in [oxidized-frying-oils] and [cooking-oil-choice]. Two human findings matter for scoring. A breakfast made with sunflower oil heated through 20 frying cycles lowered glutathione and raised protein carbonyls, NADPH-oxidase expression and a DNA-oxidation marker more than the same breakfast made with heated virgin olive oil or antioxidant-enriched oil [fox-perezherrera2013, fox-rangelzuniga2017]. But a double-blind trial of meals rich in olive or safflower oil used for 8 hours of deep frying found no change in endothelial function [fox-williams2001], and fresh and repeatedly fried oils impaired it equally, suggesting the fat load mattered more than the oil's oxidation [fox-ruedaclausen2007]. Cohorts disagree on fried food and heart disease [fox-qin2021, fox-guallarcastillon2012].

### Fish is the exception that proves the marker problem

Long-chain omega-3 fats are the most oxidisable fats in the diet, yet 3.4 g/day of fish-oil EPA+DHA did not raise plasma F2-isoprostanes or MDA [fox-higdon2000], and a daily fish meal lowered urinary F2-isoprostanes by 20% [fox-mori1999]. The gastric model's sharp MDA rise with fish oil [fox-tirosh2015] does not translate into whole-body lipid peroxidation in trials (see [omega3-fish]). Rancid capsules are a separate issue ([fish-oil-oxidation]). For scoring, cooked fish's PUFA content should not count as oxidant load.

**Verdict.** Dietary oxidised fat is absorbed and transiently raises blood markers, but the evidence is single meals in 5-20 people, mostly from one group, using an artefact-prone marker [fox-tsikas2017], and an expert reviewer judged the oral toxicity of oxidised lipids "unexpectedly low" [fox-esterbauer1993]. It warrants a small weight, concentrated on clear hot spots: reused frying oil, powdered or stored and reheated animal foods, ghee, aged grated cheese.

## 3. Heme iron

### Mechanism and human biomarker trials

Heme from myoglobin catalyses oxidation of dietary fat in the gut and promotes formation of N-nitroso compounds. In a randomised human crossover, adding heme iron to a diet doubled urinary DHN-MA, the main breakdown product of 4-hydroxynonenal; urinary isoprostanes did not rise [fox-pierre2006]. In metabolic-suite feeding studies, faecal N-nitroso compounds rose in a straight line with red meat from 0 to 420 g/day (about 2.6 ug/day more per gram of meat), while 420-600 g/day of white meat had no effect [fox-bingham2002]. Fresh red meat and nitrite-cured meat (366-420 g/day) both raised faecal N-nitroso compounds about 50-fold over a vegetarian diet; cured meat also raised oxidised DNA bases in cells exposed to faecal water [fox-joosen2009]. Cured meat (180 g/day for 4 days) raised faecal N-nitroso and lipid-oxidation markers, which calcium carbonate normalised [fox-pierre2013].

### Epidemiology

People with the highest heme intake have about 18% more colon cancer [fox-bastide2011]; per 1 mg/day of heme, colorectal cancer risk rises about 8-11% [fox-qiao2013, fox-fonsecanunes2014] and type 2 diabetes about 16% [fox-bao2012]. In NIH-AARP, heme (HR 1.13) and nitrate from processed meat (HR 1.16) were candidate explanations for the red and processed meat link [fox-cross2010].

### What doesn't fit

In an 8-week RCT, eating about 200 g/day of lean red meat in place of carbohydrate foods did not raise plasma F2-isoprostanes and lowered urinary F2-isoprostanes [fox-hodgson2007]. A meta-analysis of 24 RCTs found no effect of red meat on glucose, insulin or CRP [fox-oconnor2021]. The heme effect appears to be a gut-lumen effect, not systemic oxidative stress.

### Heme per serving

| Food (85 g cooked unless stated) | Heme iron, mg per serving | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Beef steak or roast | about 0.8-1.3 | measured cooked steak 9.2 ug/g [fox-cross2012]; 64-77% of total iron raw [fox-valenzuela2009, fox-pretorius2016] |
| Ground beef patty | about 0.9-1.7 | hamburger 10.3 ug/g [fox-cross2012] |
| Lamb | about 0.7-1.3 | 81% of raw iron as heme [fox-pretorius2016] |
| Pork loin or chop | about 0.3-0.6 | chop 3.4 ug/g [fox-cross2012]; 88% raw [fox-pretorius2016] |
| Chicken thigh / breast | about 0.25-0.55 / 0.15-0.25 | [fox-cross2012] |
| Bacon (3 slices, 24 g) / deli ham (56 g) | about 0.07-0.15 / 0.24-0.42 | [fox-cross2012] |
| Beef liver | about 0.7 | liver iron only about 14% heme [fox-valenzuela2009] |
| Fish | wide: 18-93% of total iron | [fox-wheal2016]; e.g. salmon under 0.3 mg, sardines up to about 2 mg |

Cooking converts part of the heme to non-heme iron, which is why cooked-meat values [fox-cross2012] run lower than raw-meat fractions. Poultry and fish carry some heme, but white meat did not raise faecal N-nitroso compounds [fox-bingham2002] and the cancer associations are with red meat, so the rubric counts heme only from mammalian meat and organ meat.

**Verdict.** Moderate evidence for a gut-level oxidant and N-nitroso effect with a modest cancer association. It earns a real but capped weight per serving of red meat. The general-health entry for [unprocessed-red-meat] summarises how weak the disease evidence is overall [fox-lescinsky2022, fox-johnston2019].

## 4. Processed meat: nitrite and N-nitroso compounds

The International Agency for Research on Cancer classes processed meat as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1), with colorectal cancer risk up about 18% per 50 g/day [fox-bouvard2015]. Processed meat brings three things at once: heme, nitrite that forms nitrosyl heme and N-nitroso compounds in the gut [fox-joosen2009, fox-pierre2013], and preformed nitrosamines. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) set an acceptable daily intake of 0.07 mg nitrite/kg body weight, found additive exposure within it, but found margins of exposure to preformed nitrosamines in meat products below the 10,000 level of comfort at high intake [fox-efsa2017nitrite]. In 2023 EFSA concluded that dietary N-nitrosamine exposure raises a health concern for all age groups, with meat and meat products the main contributors [fox-efsa2023nitrosamines]. In EPIC-Norfolk, dietary NDMA was associated with rectal cancer (HR 1.46 per SD) [fox-loh2011].

The counterpoint is that the body makes N-nitroso compounds itself: the DFG food-safety commission argues endogenous NDMA may exceed dietary exposure by 100- to 1,000-fold, so diet-only margins of exposure are incomplete [fox-eisenbrand2024]. NutriRECS rated the certainty for processed-meat harms as low [fox-johnston2019]. Even so, processed meat has the most consistent human evidence of any food component here apart from alcohol, and it should carry the largest per-serving weight among the meat components. The site's sperm data point the same way ([processed-meat]).

Mitigation is large in food-chemistry terms: 1-hour spice marinades cut HCAs in grilled steak 57-88% [fox-smith2008]; a marinade cut PhIP in grilled chicken 92-99%, although MeIQx rose with a sugary marinade at long grilling times [fox-salmon1997]; microwaving patties 1-3 minutes before frying cut HCAs 3- to 9-fold [fox-felton1994]; and a black-beer marinade cut PAHs 53% [fox-viegas2014]. Removing char and stopping fat dripping onto flames lower PAHs [fox-duedahlolesen2022]. Air-frying cut acrylamide 90% in one study [fox-sansano2015] but made no difference in another, where home-fried potatoes were all low [fox-navruzvarli2023].

In people, the top HCA intakes go with about 15-20% higher odds of colorectal adenoma [fox-martinezgongora2019], but the 165,717-person Multiethnic Cohort found no link for meat or HCAs [fox-ollberding2012]. Brewed coffee contributes little acrylamide per cup: at the 400 ug/kg benchmark and about 10 g of grounds, at most about 4 ug.

## 6. Postprandial oxidative stress from fat load and meal size

The site's glycemic category already covers sugar spikes, so this section is about fat and meal size. A 900 kcal breakfast with 50 g of fat cut flow-mediated dilation from 20% to 8-12% for 2-4 hours; the same calories with no fat did not [fox-plotnick1997]. Two hours after a high-fat meal, white-cell superoxide production rose 34% and dilation fell from 13.7% to 8.2%, with the two changes tightly correlated [fox-bae2001]. Among isocaloric meals, only the fat meal raised hydrogen peroxide and MDA [fox-fisherwellman2010]. In a direct dose comparison, 66 g of fat raised MDA and H2O2 but 33 g did not [fox-bloomer2010]. Fat and glucose loads add up [fox-ceriello2004]. A 131-study meta-analysis confirms a transient fall in dilation of about 1 percentage point at 2-4 hours [fox-fewkes2022].

Polyphenol-rich foods with the meal blunted these responses: orange juice with a 900 kcal high-fat high-carbohydrate meal prevented rises in NADPH-oxidase, leukocyte ROS and endotoxin [fox-ghanim2010]; a strawberry drink lowered post-meal oxidised LDL and triglycerides [fox-burtonfreeman2010]; and adding balsamic vinegar and salad to an olive-oil meal removed its effect on dilation [fox-vogel2000]. Postprandial oxidative stress as a concept is reviewed in [fox-sies2005].

**Verdict.** Moderate acute evidence, unknown long-term significance. A threshold of about 30 g fat per serving for a small penalty and 50 g for a larger one fits the dose data.

## 7. Alcohol and contaminants

**Alcohol.** In a double-blind, placebo-controlled study, acute alcohol raised urinary isoprostanes in step with blood alcohol [fox-meagher1999]. Four weeks of four glasses of red wine a day (41 g alcohol) raised urinary 8-iso-PGF2alpha compared with dealcoholised wine, so the polyphenols did not cancel the alcohol [fox-schrieks2013]. Mortality is lowest at or below about 100 g/week [fox-wood2018], and bias-adjusted meta-analysis finds no protective effect of light drinking [fox-zhao2023]. This is the most direct human evidence of any food component that one serving raises systemic oxidative stress. See [alcohol].

**Metals.** Methylmercury, cadmium, inorganic arsenic and lead all deplete glutathione or bind thiols [fox-jomova2011]. At ordinary dietary levels, though, the evidence that a serving of food raises oxidative stress is thin:

- **Methylmercury:** fish benefits outweigh risks except for a few high-mercury species [fox-mozaffarian2006]; see [mercury-fish].
- **Inorganic arsenic:** EFSA's 2024 margins of exposure from rice and other foods are low enough to raise concern [fox-efsa2024arsenic], but no study shows that ordinary rice meals change oxidative markers ([arsenic-rice]).
- **Cadmium:** 16% of young European adults exceed urinary alert values [fox-snojtratnik2022]; cocoa is one dietary source ([cocoa-heavy-metals]).
- **Lead in spices:** adulteration is a sporadic poisoning risk rather than a per-serving oxidant load ([lead-spices]).

The honest treatment is a small per-serving point only for "avoid" high-mercury fish, with the rest kept as watch-outs ([heavy-metals]).

## 8. Mitigation by meal composition

The most practical finding on this page is that what you eat with meat changes how much oxidised fat you absorb. The best-supported moves, from human meal studies:

- **Herbs and spices in the meat:** a spice blend cut MDA 71% in the meat and 49% in urine [fox-li2010]. Spice marinades cut HCAs 57-88% [fox-smith2008]. See [herbs-spices].
- **Polyphenol drinks or foods with the meal:** wine polyphenols [fox-gorelik2008a], roasted coffee [fox-sirota2013], berries [fox-urquiaga2017, fox-burtonfreeman2010] and orange juice [fox-ghanim2010]. Wine is not a sensible choice because of its alcohol.
- **Vegetables and vinegar:** salad with balsamic vinegar [fox-vogel2000]; in a gastric model, plant foods protected in proportion to their polyphenol content [fox-kanner2017].
- **Acid marinades:** lemon juice or vinegar halved AGE formation [fox-uribarri2010] and cut HCAs [fox-salmon1997]. See [marinating-meat].
- **Calcium with cured meat** normalised faecal markers in one trial using calcium carbonate [fox-pierre2013]; whether dairy in the meal does the same is untested.

This is plausible because unabsorbed polyphenols reach high concentrations in the gut, unlike in blood [fox-halliwell2000]. But each trial is one meal in 10-24 people, and a month of polyphenol-enriched burgers lowered oxidised LDL without changing plasma MDA [fox-urquiaga2018]. See [meal-polyphenol-pairing].

## Sperm and egg relevance

Little of this has been tested directly on sperm or eggs. On the site, processed meat is the meat signal linked to worse semen while unprocessed red meat is not ([processed-meat], [unprocessed-red-meat]), and alcohol harms both ([alcohol]). No human study has tested dietary AGEs, heme or oxidised fats on sperm DNA fragmentation or egg quality. The fertility categories on the Foods and Recipes pages should keep scoring those outcomes separately.

## Where the evidence is contested

- **Dietary AGEs, all of it.** Open-label trials from one group, plus two independent blinded trials, found metabolic or oxidative benefits of low-AGE diets [fox-vlassara2016, fox-decourten2016, fox-mark2014, fox-baye2017]. The most rigorous double-blind trial [fox-linkens2022], large cohorts [fox-linkens2023, fox-aglago2021, fox-sharifizahabi2024, fox-ahmadirad2025] and a formal food-safety review [fox-hellwig2024] found nothing.
- **Which AGE database is right.** ELISA [fox-uribarri2010] and mass spectrometry [fox-scheijen2016] disagree on whether fats and butter are high- or low-AGE foods. Method reviewers side with the need for validated instrumental methods [fox-ames2008, fox-poulsen2013, fox-charissou2007].
- **Postprandial MDA.** Kanner's group finds large, polyphenol-preventable MDA rises after meat [fox-gorelik2008a, fox-sirota2013], replicated in outline by others [fox-li2010, fox-urquiaga2017]. Critics note that MDA assays are artefact-prone [fox-tsikas2017] and that oxidised lipids show low oral toxicity [fox-esterbauer1993]. A long-used frying oil meal left endothelial function unchanged [fox-williams2001].
- **Heme and systemic oxidative stress.** Heme clearly raises gut lipid-oxidation and N-nitroso markers [fox-pierre2006, fox-bingham2002], but lean red meat did not raise isoprostanes [fox-hodgson2007] and red meat RCTs show no inflammatory effect [fox-oconnor2021]. Disease evidence for unprocessed red meat is rated weak [fox-lescinsky2022, fox-johnston2019], and one major cohort was null [fox-ollberding2012].
- **Nitrosamines.** EFSA sees a health concern [fox-efsa2023nitrosamines]; the DFG commission argues endogenous formation dwarfs dietary exposure [fox-eisenbrand2024].
- **Fat load versus oxidised fat.** Whether the oxidation state of a fat matters acutely, or only its amount, is unresolved: fresh and fried oils impaired dilation equally [fox-ruedaclausen2007], but heated sunflower oil raised oxidative markers more than heated olive oil [fox-perezherrera2013]. The 90-study meta-analysis even found smaller dilation falls with higher-fat meals [fox-fewkes2022].
- **Glucose and postprandial oxidative stress.** A 75 g glucose drink more than doubled leukocyte ROS in one study [fox-mohanty2000] but produced minimal change in another [fox-bloomer2010]. That is why the glycemic side stays in its own category.
- **Fish oil in the stomach.** A gastric model shows fish oil multiplying meat peroxidation [fox-tirosh2015], while human trials of fish oil and fish meals show no rise, or a fall, in isoprostanes [fox-higdon2000, fox-mori1999].
- **Air-frying and acrylamide.** A 90% reduction in one study [fox-sansano2015], no difference in another [fox-navruzvarli2023].
- **HCAs and cancer.** Adenoma meta-analysis positive [fox-martinezgongora2019]; Multiethnic Cohort null [fox-ollberding2012].

## Practical bottom line

1. **Don't drink for antioxidants.** Alcohol is the clearest per-serving oxidant load in the diet.
2. **Keep processed meat occasional.** It combines heme, nitrite, nitrosamines and usually high-heat cooking.
3. **Eat red meat in modest portions and cook it gently.** Stew, braise or roast at moderate heat, and avoid charring. Poultry, fish and legumes carry much less heme.
4. **Season and accompany meat.** Use herbs and spices in the meat or the marinade, and serve it with vegetables, fruit, coffee or tea.
5. **Watch hot spots of oxidised fat:** reused frying oil, powdered egg or milk, ghee, and cooked meat stored and reheated.
6. **Avoid huge fatty meals.** About 50 g of fat or more in one sitting transiently impairs blood vessels.
7. **Don't fear fish for its omega-3s, or butter for its "AGEs".** Neither claim holds up on the best data.

## What we don't know

- Whether any of these transient post-meal markers predict disease, and whether reducing them over years helps.
- Real per-serving AGE exposure, since the open-access mass-spectrometry data are category ranges and absorption varies by compound.
- Heme content of cooked fish and shellfish in Western species (PubMed-indexed data found here are from Bangladeshi species), and of bivalves at all.
- Whether calcium-rich foods, rather than calcium carbonate, blunt heme and nitrite effects.
- Whether meal pairing works over months, not just hours.
- Oxysterol content of everyday foods with harmonised methods.

## How this feeds the score

The proposed rubric (load-rubric-proposal.md in the scratchpad hand-off) builds a 1-10 "Oxidant load" per serving from these components, with the largest weights for alcohol and processed meat, medium weights for high-heat meat cooking and red-meat heme, and small weights for oxidised fat hot spots, browned starches (acrylamide plus AGEs), fat load and high-mercury fish, minus up to 2 points for herbs, spices and produce eaten with the meat. It leaves sugar spikes to the existing glycemic category and absorbs the old recipe cooking category.
